## Evidence-Backed Extraction

- Review question: Which nonpharmacological intervention types have evidence of benefit for informal dementia caregiver outcomes, and what methodological issues explain conflicting review conclusions?
- Inclusion logic: Included peer-reviewed English systematic reviews and/or meta-analyses published from 2006-2018 that reported intervention outcomes for informal caregivers of community- or home-based people with dementia. Excluded pharmacological interventions, process evaluations, scoping reviews, institutionalized-care-recipient contexts, and care-recipient-only interventions without caregiver involvement.
- Search/screening: MEDLINE, PsycINFO, CINAHL, and Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews; 243 titles/abstracts screened, 115 full papers inspected, 60 reviews selected after exclusions.
- Evidence scope: The included reviews covered more than 500 nonoverlapping intervention studies; most reviews were rated low quality by AMSTAR II, although the authors did not find review quality factors systematically explaining conclusions.
- Intervention taxonomy: Psychoeducation, counseling/psychotherapy, occupational therapy, mindfulness-based interventions, communication skills training, respite/day care, home care/support, physical activity, technology-based interventions, multicomponent interventions, dyadic interventions, and miscellaneous categories.
- Outcome taxonomy: Burden, depression, anxiety, quality of life/well-being, mastery/self-efficacy/competence, social support, communication knowledge/skills, and care-recipient-related outcomes where reviewed.
- Main findings: Depression was the most modifiable outcome, with strongest support for psychoeducation, counseling/psychotherapy, mindfulness, and multicomponent interventions. Evidence also supported quality of life through psychoeducation, mastery through psychoeducation/occupational therapy/multicomponent interventions, and communication skills through communication training.
- Weak or null areas: Anxiety and social support had little clear intervention evidence; burden evidence was mixed or weak; support groups and respite were generally ineffective in this review-level synthesis.
- Review-level takeaway: Intervention effects are domain-specific. Matching the intervention mechanism to the intended caregiver outcome matters more than assuming multicomponent, dyadic, group, or technology delivery is inherently superior.
- What the review does not answer: It does not identify a single best intervention package, prove broad effects for all caregiver outcomes, establish AI or smart-home effectiveness, or resolve active-component questions because intervention categories and outcome definitions were inconsistent across prior reviews.

## Review-Level Takeaway and Story

### Why this review exists

The dementia caregiver intervention literature had accumulated many systematic reviews and meta-analyses, but their conclusions were hard to reconcile because intervention labels, outcome definitions, and primary-study groupings varied. Cheng and Zhang use a meta-review to ask what can still be said at the review-of-reviews level.

### What the review is really about

The review is not just a list of dementia caregiver interventions. Its central contribution is a sorting function: it separates intervention type, active mechanism, and caregiver outcome domain so that future claims do not collapse all caregiver support into a generic burden-reduction promise.

### What it lets the wiki say

- Dementia caregiver intervention evidence is strongest when claims are outcome-specific.
- Depression is a more consistently modifiable caregiver outcome than burden, anxiety, or social support in this review-level synthesis.
- Psychoeducation, counseling/psychotherapy, mindfulness, communication training, occupational therapy, and multicomponent interventions support different claims and should not be treated as interchangeable.
- Intervention package labels are weaker than mechanism-plus-outcome mapping for system design.

### What it does not let the wiki say

- It does not prove that any single dementia caregiver intervention is broadly best.
- It does not establish that technology delivery, dyadic format, respite, support groups, or multicomponent packaging is inherently superior.
- It does not support AI-agent, chatbot, smart-home, or dementia clinical treatment claims.

### Design interpretation

For caregiver-system design, Cheng 2020 should function as the outcome-matching backbone. A future AI or digital caregiver system should first specify whether it targets knowledge, communication skill, mastery, depression, quality of life, burden, anxiety, or social support, then choose content, coaching, referral, or interaction patterns that plausibly match that endpoint.

## Gaps and Cautions

- Do not treat dementia caregiver intervention evidence as dementia treatment evidence.
- Do not treat technology-based intervention findings as AI-agent or smart-home evidence.
- Do not treat usability, delivery modality, or intervention complexity as effectiveness by itself.
- Classifications across reviews were inconsistent; source-backed claims should carry taxonomy and outcome-domain caveats.
