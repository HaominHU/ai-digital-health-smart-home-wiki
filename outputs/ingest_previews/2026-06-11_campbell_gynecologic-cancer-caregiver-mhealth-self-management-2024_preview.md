## Evidence-Backed Extraction

- Problem/gap: Family caregivers of people with gynecologic cancer experience distress and unmet support needs. Web-based caregiver support interventions have evidence but may lack portability; mHealth could support patient-caregiver dyads if designed around real user needs, privacy, and information-sharing preferences.
- Study objective: The study sought information on desired usage and features for designing an mHealth self-management support app targeting both patients with gynecologic cancer and their caregivers.
- Population/condition scope: Women treated for gynecologic cancers, including ovarian, fallopian, primary peritoneal, uterine, endometrial, cervical, and vulvar cancers, plus self-identified closest support persons or caregivers.
- Setting: Recruitment from a large quaternary care, university-affiliated health system in Western Pennsylvania, using a cancer registry at an NCI-designated cancer center and satellite network.
- Methods: Eight Zoom focus groups were conducted over six weeks using a HIPAA-compliant Zoom account. Groups lasted about 90 minutes. A semistructured guide elicited gaps in information/support, desired mobile-app content and features, and preferences for individual or dyadic use.
- Sample: Final sample was 63 participants: 41 individuals with gynecologic cancer and 22 caregivers/support persons. Patients were aged 32-84 years, mostly White and married. Caregivers were aged 19-81 years; 68% identified as male, 32% female, and 59% were spouses.
- Analysis: Transcripts were analyzed using rapid qualitative analysis and a thematic matrix. Four investigators coded early transcripts, adjudicated conflicts, and reached a mean Cohen kappa of 0.80 before remaining transcripts were coded by validated coders.
- Main topic 1, gaps in information and support: Patients and caregivers reported that finding relevant information and resources was time-consuming, frustrating, and difficult because information found independently often lacked applicability, trustworthiness, or clarity.
- Self-triage gap: Patients and caregivers lacked confidence in deciding the urgency of new symptoms or concerns and in knowing whom to contact, especially outside clinic hours. Caregivers wanted reassurance about what was normal versus concerning.
- Main topic 2, desired mHealth features: Participants wanted centralized, curated, trustworthy information from gynecologic oncology experts; plain-language glossaries; cancer- and treatment-specific content; and anticipatory caregiver information about what to expect during chemotherapy, radiation, and symptom management.
- Tailoring and trajectory: Participants wanted timely recommendations tailored to personal needs, cancer type, treatment phase, and survivorship concerns. Reminder preferences varied, so customization and changeable reminder frequency were important.
- Professional and peer interaction: Participants wanted opportunities to interact with knowledgeable clinical experts and peer experts through the app, including moderated question-answer or chat room features to preserve accuracy and support.
- Main topic 3, app usage preferences: Patients and caregivers wanted private spaces to seek information, express concerns, and obtain support separately from the other dyad member. They also wanted granular control over what information could be shared with caregivers, patients, and other family members.
- Novel findings emphasized by the authors: The study highlights self-triage support for symptom urgency and dyadic privacy/sharing control as important design requirements for gynecologic cancer mHealth self-management support.
- Design implication: A single app for both patients and caregivers should not assume universal sharing or always-dyadic use. It should allow flexible individual, dyadic, and family-sharing modes, with user-controlled privacy and sharing.
- HCD/accessibility details: Participants noted variable technology proficiency and suggested alternative support routes such as call centers for people who are not tech-savvy. The authors caution that the mostly White sample limits generalizability to non-White and rural-dwelling individuals.
- Evidence limit: This is formative qualitative design evidence and perceived acceptability/interest evidence, not mHealth effectiveness evidence.

## Full-Study Takeaway

### What this study is really about

Campbell 2024 is direct gynecologic cancer dyad design evidence. It shows that patients and caregivers want mHealth support for curated information, symptom-urgency self-triage, trajectory-tailored recommendations, expert/peer connection, and privacy-aware information sharing. The study's strongest contribution is not proving that an app works; it clarifies what a future gynecologic cancer patient-caregiver app must handle to be usable and acceptable.

### What it lets the wiki say

- Gynecologic cancer patients and caregivers may need curated, trustworthy, plain-language, trajectory-specific information.
- Caregivers may need anticipatory guidance on what to expect during treatment and how to support the patient.
- Symptom urgency and "who should I contact" decisions are important self-management support gaps.
- Patient-caregiver apps need private spaces and granular sharing controls for both members of the dyad.
- Peer and clinical expert interaction are desired, but accuracy, moderation, and trusted expertise matter.

### What it does not let the wiki say

- It does not establish mHealth effectiveness for gynecologic cancer caregivers.
- It does not prove that recommender algorithms improve outcomes.
- It does not evaluate AI, smart-home sensing, autonomous triage, or clinical decision support.
- It does not represent all racial, rural, socioeconomic, or technology-access groups.
- It should not be generalized to all cancer caregiver populations without condition and setting caveats.

## Gaps and Cautions

- This is direct gynecologic cancer evidence, but it is formative qualitative design evidence rather than intervention-effectiveness evidence.
- Sample was mostly White and recruited through a trusted gynecologic oncology practice; generalizability to non-White, rural, lower-resource, and lower-digital-literacy groups is limited.
- Focus groups included both patients and caregivers, which may have inhibited disclosure of some private caregiver or patient concerns.
- COVID-19 timing may have shaped interest in mHealth and remote support.
- Self-triage support should be designed as guidance and escalation support, not autonomous clinical decision-making.
- Do not treat desired AI/recommender functionality as evaluated AI evidence.
