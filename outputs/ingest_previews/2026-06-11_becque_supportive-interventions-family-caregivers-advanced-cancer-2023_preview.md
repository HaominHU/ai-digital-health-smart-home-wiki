## Evidence-Backed Extraction

- Review question/objective: The review systematically examined characteristics of supportive interventions for family caregivers of patients with advanced cancer, evidence for intervention effectiveness across caregiver outcome dimensions, and whether intervention characteristics were associated with caregiver outcomes.
- Inclusion logic: Included English-language quantitative studies published from January 2004 through January 2020 that reported interventions to support adult family caregivers of patients with advanced cancer and included family caregiver well-being outcomes.
- Exclusion logic: Excluded patient-focused interventions rather than caregiver-focused interventions, bereavement-specific interventions, pediatric cancer or mental comorbidity/cognitive impairment contexts such as dementia, conference abstracts, and pilot or feasibility studies.
- Search/screening: Searches used Embase, Medline Ovid, Web of Science, Cochrane Central, CINAHL, and Google Scholar. The review identified 16,713 records; after duplicate removal, 7,957 records remained; 87 full-text publications were reviewed; 35 articles reporting 32 studies met inclusion criteria.
- Study designs: Twenty-two included studies were randomized controlled trials. The remainder included quasi-experimental, stepped-wedge cluster non-randomized, single-group pre-post, and other quantitative designs.
- Population/condition scope: Family caregivers of adults with advanced cancer across care settings. Most studies included patients with varied cancer diagnoses; a smaller number focused on lung cancer or breast cancer. This is broad advanced cancer caregiver evidence, not gynecological-cancer-specific evidence.
- Caregiver relationship scope: Spouses were the most common family caregiver group, but adult children, parents, and other family members were also represented.
- Quality appraisal: Included studies were appraised with the Hawker Quality Assessment Tool. The average score was 28.4 on a 9-36 scale, with scores ranging from 22 to 33. Ethical issues were reported less often than other quality domains.
- Intervention target-population taxonomy: Interventions targeted individual family caregivers, patient-caregiver dyads, families, or peer groups. Counts in the abstract were individual caregivers (n = 15), dyads (n = 11), families (n = 2), and peer groups (n = 4).
- Intervention content taxonomy: Intervention components were classified as patient caregiving, family caregiver self-care, and family care. Most interventions were multicomponent. Thirty-one interventions included caregiver self-care, 22 included patient-caregiving support, and 16 included family-care content.
- Theory and mechanism: Eleven interventions were based on Lazarus and Folkman's stress and coping theory; other interventions used diverse theoretical bases. This makes the source useful for coping/stress-process design rationale in advanced cancer caregiving.
- Delivery modes and settings: Most interventions were delivered face-to-face, sometimes combined with telephone contacts. Three interventions used telephone contacts only, and two were online/user-driven eHealth interventions. Many interventions were home-based; others occurred in outpatient hospitals, inpatient hospitals, palliative care units, hospice, or general-practitioner office settings.
- Outcome taxonomy: Caregiver outcomes were classified into physical, psycho-emotional, social, spiritual, daily functioning, and quality of life dimensions.
- Main findings: Most interventions significantly improved at least one family caregiver outcome. The abstract reports 29 interventions with beneficial effects, mostly in psycho-emotional outcomes (n = 24), daily functioning (n = 13), and social outcomes (n = 6).
- Target-population pattern: Individual interventions mainly improved psycho-emotional outcomes. Dyadic and family interventions improved psycho-emotional and social outcomes. Group interventions mainly improved daily functioning.
- Delivery-mode caution: The review did not find that mode, duration, dose, provider, setting, or content category clearly explained outcomes. Face-to-face care may matter for nonverbal and affective communication, but the review also found positive effects from online and telephone interventions.
- Clinical/practice implication: Healthcare organizations should select interventions that match family caregivers' problems, needs, preferences, and context, while also considering organizational and financial consequences such as session number, duration, provider-driven versus self-administered format, and delivery setting.
- Review-level takeaway: Becque 2023 is an advanced cancer caregiver intervention taxonomy and outcome-mapping source. It does not identify one universally superior intervention; instead, it suggests that intervention target group and outcome dimension matter, especially when deciding whether support should focus on individual caregivers, dyads/families, or peer groups.
- What the review does not answer: It does not provide gynecological-cancer-specific intervention evidence, does not establish AI-agent or smart-home effectiveness, does not prove digital delivery is equivalent or superior to face-to-face support, and does not isolate active intervention components because included studies were heterogeneous.

## Review-Level Takeaway and Story

### Why this review exists

Advanced cancer caregiving is intensive, often occurring in palliative and end-of-life contexts where family caregivers provide ADL support, medical-care support, household and financial help, psychosocial support, communication support, and advocacy. Many supportive interventions existed, but their populations, delivery modes, components, and outcome targets differed enough that a simple "does support work" answer was not sufficient.

### What the review is really about

This review is a sorting and matching source for advanced cancer caregiver support. It organizes interventions by who is targeted, what component is offered, where and how it is delivered, and which caregiver outcome dimension changes. The review's practical contribution is not a single best program; it is a map for matching support format to caregiver need and outcome domain.

### What it lets the wiki say

- Advanced cancer caregiver interventions are diverse and often multicomponent.
- Most reviewed interventions reported at least one beneficial caregiver outcome, especially psycho-emotional outcomes.
- Individual caregiver interventions, dyadic/family interventions, and group interventions appear to align with different outcome dimensions.
- Caregiver self-care is a dominant component in this literature, but patient-caregiving and family-care components are also common.
- Advanced cancer caregiver intervention design should account for caregiver context, relationship structure, delivery burden, organizational capacity, and outcome domain.

### What it does not let the wiki say

- It does not prove that any one intervention model is best for all advanced cancer caregivers.
- It does not provide gynecological-cancer-specific intervention effects.
- It does not establish effectiveness of AI agents, smart-home systems, mHealth, or autonomous caregiver support.
- It does not show that usability, acceptability, online delivery, or telephone delivery is the same as effectiveness.
- It does not replace Hartnett 2016 for end-stage ovarian cancer caregiver burden.

### Design interpretation

For caregiver-system design, Becque 2023 should function as an advanced cancer caregiver support taxonomy. A future digital or AI-supported caregiver system should first identify the caregiver's dominant support target: psycho-emotional strain, daily caregiving competence, social/family functioning, spiritual meaning, physical strain, or quality of life. It should then choose whether the support should be individual, dyadic, family-oriented, or group/peer-oriented. The source supports design logic for matching intervention format to caregiver need, but not claims that a digital system is effective.

## Gaps and Cautions

- The review is broad advanced cancer caregiver evidence, not gynecological-cancer-specific evidence.
- Included studies were heterogeneous in intervention content, target group, delivery mode, dose, setting, and outcome measurement.
- The review's own synthesis says target population appeared associated with outcome dimension, while other intervention characteristics were not clearly associated with outcomes.
- Most studies were conducted in Western-oriented countries, limiting transferability to non-Western care contexts.
- Ethical issues were less frequently reported in the included studies.
- Table extraction from the two-column PDF is artifact-prone; use narrative results for integration unless exact table fields are manually checked.
- Do not treat telephone or online intervention signals as evidence for AI-agent, smart-home, or mHealth effectiveness.
- Do not treat supportive-care intervention evidence as cancer treatment evidence or clinical decision support.
