# Glasgow 2001 RE-AIM Framework for Chronic Illness Management

## Summary

Glasgow et al. 2001 applies the RE-AIM framework to chronic illness management interventions. The article argues that intervention planning and funding should consider Reach, Efficacy, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance rather than relying only on efficacy in randomized trials.

## Source-Backed Findings

- RE-AIM evaluates interventions across five dimensions: Reach, Efficacy, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance.
- Reach concerns the proportion and representativeness of the target population participating in the intervention.
- Efficacy concerns intervention success when implemented as intended.
- Adoption concerns the proportion and representativeness of settings, practices, or plans that adopt the intervention.
- Implementation concerns the quality and consistency of delivery in real-world settings.
- Maintenance concerns sustained behavior change at the individual level and institutionalization at the organizational level.
- The article compares one-to-one counseling, group sessions, telephone interventions, interactive computer interventions, mailed print, and health system policies.
- The authors argue that interventions with strong efficacy among participants may still have limited population impact if reach, adoption, implementation, or maintenance are weak.
- Traditional face-to-face and hospital-based modalities can face barriers such as referral requirements, scheduling, transportation, cost, and limited scalability.
- Telephone, automated, interactive computer, mailed, and policy modalities may offer stronger reach, consistency, adoption, or maintenance depending on the setting.
- Policy changes received high ratings across multiple RE-AIM dimensions because they can affect the care context broadly.

## Wiki-Relevant Interpretation

For this wiki, RE-AIM is most useful as an evaluation and implementation framework for caregiver-support, digital health, smart-home, and chronic illness management interventions. It helps prevent over-weighting usability, acceptability, or efficacy findings while ignoring who was reached, which organizations adopted the intervention, whether it was delivered consistently, and whether it lasted.

## Design and Evaluation Uses

- Use reach to track who is included or excluded by technology access, broadband, device ownership, literacy, disability, scheduling, referral pathways, transportation, and caregiver workload.
- Use efficacy/effectiveness to track caregiver, care-recipient, utilization, economic, safety, and quality-of-life outcomes.
- Use adoption to track whether clinics, care teams, community organizations, homes, or payers can realistically take up the intervention.
- Use implementation to track fidelity, workflow fit, usability in practice, support burden, data completeness, and protocol consistency.
- Use maintenance to track sustained caregiver/care-recipient use and whether organizations institutionalize the intervention.

## Evidence Limits

- The article is not caregiver-specific.
- The intervention modality ratings are author consensus ratings, not a systematic meta-analysis.
- Technology access assumptions reflect the 2000-era context and should be updated with current evidence before making present-day claims.
- This source does not establish effectiveness of any specific AI, mHealth, or smart-home system.
- The framework supports research design and evaluation, not clinical decision-making.
