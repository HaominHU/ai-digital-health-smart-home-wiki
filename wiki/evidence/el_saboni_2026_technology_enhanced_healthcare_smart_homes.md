# Technology-Enhanced Health Care in Smart Homes: Scoping Review of Sensor Technologies, Clinical Applications, Integration Challenges, and Future Directions

## Source Role

Maps smart-home sensing, architecture, applications, and implementation gaps. Use for technology and evaluation rationale; the proposed maturity taxonomy does not establish clinical readiness or caregiver benefit.

## Study or Framework Picture

- PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, and IEEE Xplore were searched for English-language, peer-reviewed studies from January 2005 through February 2025.
- The authors report 6,276 initial records and 169 included papers, published from 2005 through 2024. Eligibility covered home-based health sensing, monitoring, infrastructure, analytics, and implementation; exclusively hospital-based work and health-unrelated home automation were excluded.
- Experimental, qualitative, and system-design work were synthesized across technical, clinical, and sociotechnical domains. No formal methodological quality or risk-of-bias appraisal was performed.
- The scope spans older adults and chronic-condition care, but does not constitute a condition-specific caregiver review.

## Main Findings and Contribution

- Primary themes were sensor technologies (68/169, 40.2%), Internet of Things infrastructure (34/169, 20.1%), machine learning (27/169, 16.0%), telehealth (24/169, 14.2%), and ethics/usability (16/169, 9.5%). These counts describe literature emphasis.
- Sensing covers cardiovascular, respiratory, neurological, metabolic, and mobility parameters through wearable, environmental, and specialized devices. Analytics applications include activity recognition, anomaly detection, and predictive modeling; evaluation of an algorithm remains distinct from clinical outcome evaluation.
- The authors propose foundational, connected, and intelligent smart-home stages to organize automation, networked coordination, and adaptive analytics. The model is conceptual, with no validated quantitative readiness score.
- Clinical workflow integration, interoperability, representative user testing, regulatory evaluation, and sustainable operation receive less attention than sensors and infrastructure. Deployment is geographically concentrated in higher-income settings.
- The review discusses benefits reported by individual studies, alongside privacy, literacy, affordability, reliability, and ongoing support barriers. Its main contribution is a broad integration map and evaluation agenda, not a pooled effect estimate.

## Evidence Limits and Conceptual Boundaries

- Heterogeneous designs were neither quality-weighted nor meta-analyzed. The review cannot establish comparative performance, causation, or a general clinical benefit of smart homes.
- Broad outcome statements in the abstract/conclusion (including hospitalization and cost) exceed what an unweighted mapping alone can confirm. Preserve them as reports from some included studies; consult the underlying studies before quantitative or effectiveness claims.
- English-only and peer-reviewed-only selection may omit local implementation knowledge. The search ended in February 2025 despite publication in September 2026.
- Technical maturity, sensor accuracy, sustained adoption, caregiver workload, safe escalation, and care-recipient outcomes are separate evidence questions. Do not infer caregiver burden reduction, diagnosis, or autonomous response validity.

## Wiki Interpretation and Design Implications

**Cross-source interpretation:** assess home systems across sensing quality, connectivity, permissions, workflow, human response capacity, and sustained use. Increased architectural complexity creates additional evaluation needs; it is not a validated path to clinical readiness.

**Design direction to test:** define false-alert and missed-alert handling, non-alert meaning, response ownership, fallback operation, consent among household members, and maintenance cost. Clinical alerts and health interpretations need separate validation and human oversight.

## Privacy and Safety

Use published aggregate results and generalized themes. Do not reproduce participant quotations or author contact information. Health and household data require consent, minimum necessary access, secure storage, retention limits, auditability, and attention to re-identification and unequal access. Proposed clinical alerts require validation, human oversight, and defined escalation responsibilities. This source is for research, design, and knowledge support.
