# Mahmood 2026 Evidence on Telehealth Use Among Informal Caregivers

## Source Role

This paper provides U.S. national survey evidence on telehealth service utilization among middle-aged and older informal caregivers. It is useful for caregiver digital support, telehealth access, multi-participant visit design, and caregiver-inclusive healthcare access policy.

It should not be treated as telehealth intervention-effectiveness evidence or AI evidence.

## Objective

The study examined the relationship between informal caregiving status and telehealth service utilization among U.S. adults aged 50+, including modes of use, motivators, and barriers during the COVID-19 pandemic period.

## Methods

- Secondary analysis of Health Information National Trends Survey 6.
- HINTS6 was fielded in 2022.
- Analytic sample: 3,354 noninstitutionalized U.S. adults aged 50+ who reported trying to schedule medical care in the prior 12 months.
- Informal caregivers were respondents currently caring for or making healthcare decisions for someone with a medical, behavioral, disability, or other condition, excluding respondents providing this care professionally.
- Telehealth utilization was categorized as received telehealth, offered but did not receive telehealth, or not offered and not received telehealth.
- Analyses used survey weights, Rao-Scott chi-square tests, and multivariable multinomial logistic regression.

## Principal Findings

- The analytic sample represented an estimated 106.1 million U.S. middle-aged and older adults in 2022.
- 42.4% reported receiving telehealth services.
- 10.3% were offered but did not use telehealth services.
- 19.8% identified as informal caregivers.
- Compared with non-caregivers, informal caregivers were significantly more likely to have received telehealth services, with adjusted odds ratio 1.78 and 95% CI 1.31-2.40.
- Among caregivers who used telehealth, video-only was the most common mode, followed by phone-only and combined video/phone use.
- Caregivers more often cited convenience and the ability to involve family members or other caregivers in appointments as reasons for telehealth use.
- Among those offered but not using telehealth, preference for in-person care was the dominant deterrent.

## Wiki Interpretation

For this wiki, the study supports caregiver-inclusive telehealth design. It suggests that telehealth systems for middle-aged and older caregivers should support convenience, scheduling flexibility, family/caregiver participation, and coordinated after-visit information.

It also supports treating caregiver status as a meaningful variable in telehealth access and utilization research, rather than assuming caregivers behave like non-caregivers of the same age.

## Evidence Limits

- Cross-sectional design cannot establish causality.
- Telehealth use may have been for caregivers' own healthcare rather than for care-recipient care.
- HINTS6 did not distinguish primary versus specialty care, service context, internet connectivity, device availability, or digital literacy barriers in enough detail.
- Findings are U.S.-specific and reflect a pandemic-period telehealth context.
- This is utilization and association evidence, not caregiver intervention-effectiveness evidence.

## What This Study Does Not Answer

- Whether telehealth reduced caregiver burden or improved care-recipient outcomes.
- Whether telehealth visits included caregivers on behalf of care recipients or were caregivers' own healthcare visits.
- Which telehealth platform features are most usable or effective for caregivers.
- Whether similar patterns persist after pandemic-era policy waivers and service disruptions.
