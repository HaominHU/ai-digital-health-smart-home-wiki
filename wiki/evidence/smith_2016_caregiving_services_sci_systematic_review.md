# Smith 2016 Caregiving Services in SCI Systematic Review

## Source Role

Smith et al. 2016 is a PRISMA-guided systematic review of caregiving services in spinal cord injury. Use it as SCI service-structure, formal/informal care, care-hours, functional predictor, caregiver/attendant training, and evidence-gap framing. Do not use it as the main SCI caregiver burden synthesis because caregiver burden studies were explicitly excluded.

## Review-Level Picture

- Review objective: Summarize and characterize literature on the nature of caregiving services for people with SCI, factors associated with caregiving service use, and interventions addressing care-recipient caregiving needs.
- Inclusion logic: Included English-language peer-reviewed qualitative and quantitative studies with at least 50% adult participants that described caregiving services in SCI, factors influencing use or access, or interventions to address caregiving needs of people with SCI.
- Exclusion boundary: Studies specifically addressing caregiver experiences and factors contributing to caregiver burden were excluded as a distinct area of study.
- Search/screening: Searched January 1980 through December 2015 across PubMed/MEDLINE, CINAHL, PsycINFO, EMBASE, Social Services Abstracts, and Social Work Abstracts. After duplicate removal, 2004 articles were identified and 16 were included.
- Evidence quality: Included study levels of evidence ranged from 2 to 5; 13 studies were level 5, indicating an overall low-quality evidence base.
- Included study types: Eleven descriptive studies and five experimental/intervention studies.
- Caregiving service definition: Home-based paid or unpaid supports that assist people with SCI with basic and instrumental ADLs, including personal care, household management, and financial management.
- Caregiver/service taxonomy: Informal caregivers included spouses, parents, siblings, children, friends, and others. Roles included ADL support, nursing care, prevention or management of secondary health conditions, housekeeping, emotional care, practical care, errands, advice, advocacy, knowledge brokerage, and financial assistance.
- Care hours and need factors: Mean care hours ranged from 2.3 to 11.6 hours per day, with individual reports from 1 to 24 hours per day. Higher neurological impairment, higher self-reported disability, lower FIM scores, and lower IADL capacity were associated with more caregiving hours.
- Informal/formal care: Caregiving services were predominantly informal and often provided by women. Perceived quality of informal care matched or exceeded formal care in reviewed studies, but rigorous quality comparisons were missing.
- Training evidence: Caregiver or attendant training was a recurring theme. Training showed preliminary knowledge gains for secondary health conditions, respiratory complications, spasticity, autonomic dysreflexia, aging effects, and community services.

## Wiki Interpretation

This review should function as the SCI caregiving-services backbone. It helps the wiki track who provides SCI care, how much care is provided, what predicts care hours, where training evidence exists, and why care-recipient independence and care quality are important design and evaluation targets.

## Design Implications

- Track formal/informal care mix, caregiver backup availability, training history, care hours, and care-recipient functional independence.
- Treat caregiver/attendant training as promising but underdeveloped, especially for secondary-condition prevention and health maintenance.
- Evaluate whether interventions reduce unnecessary caregiving hours by improving ADL/IADL independence or sustain care quality without increasing caregiver burden.
- Treat aging with SCI as a potential long-term care-demand issue rather than assuming care needs always decrease with years post-injury.
- Pair this review with caregiver burden or lived-experience sources when making claims about caregiver distress.

## Evidence Limits

- Most included studies were low-level evidence.
- Intervention evidence was limited and generally low quality.
- Studies spanned 1995-2015 and may not reflect current service models.
- English-language restriction may bias coverage.
- Heterogeneity prevented specific clinical recommendations.
- Caregiver burden and caregiver lived experience were excluded by design.
- This is not AI, smart-home, mHealth, autonomous care, or SCI disease-background evidence.
