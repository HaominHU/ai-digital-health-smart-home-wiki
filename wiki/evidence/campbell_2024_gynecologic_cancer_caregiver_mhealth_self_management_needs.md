# Campbell 2024 Gynecologic Cancer Caregiver mHealth Self-Management Needs

## Source Role

Campbell et al. 2024 is direct gynecologic cancer formative mHealth design evidence from focus groups with patients and caregivers. Use it for dyadic mHealth needs, curated information, symptom-urgency self-triage, expert and peer support, private spaces, and granular information-sharing controls. Do not use it as mHealth effectiveness evidence, AI-agent evidence, smart-home evidence, autonomous triage evidence, or cancer-treatment evidence.

## Full Study Picture

- Problem/gap: Family caregivers of people with gynecologic cancer experience distress and unmet support needs. Web-based caregiver support interventions have evidence but may lack portability; mHealth could support patient-caregiver dyads if designed around real user needs, privacy, and information-sharing preferences.
- Objective: The study sought information on desired usage and features for designing an mHealth self-management support app targeting both patients with gynecologic cancer and their caregivers.
- Population/condition scope: Women treated for gynecologic cancers, including ovarian, fallopian, primary peritoneal, uterine, endometrial, cervical, and vulvar cancers, plus self-identified closest support persons or caregivers.
- Setting: Recruitment from a large quaternary care, university-affiliated health system in Western Pennsylvania, using a cancer registry at an NCI-designated cancer center and satellite network.
- Methods: Eight Zoom focus groups were conducted over six weeks using a HIPAA-compliant Zoom account. Groups lasted about 90 minutes. A semistructured guide elicited gaps in information/support, desired mobile-app content and features, and preferences for individual or dyadic use.
- Sample: Final sample was 63 participants: 41 individuals with gynecologic cancer and 22 caregivers/support persons. Patients were aged 32-84 years, mostly White and married. Caregivers were aged 19-81 years; 68% identified as male, 32% female, and 59% were spouses.
- Analysis: Transcripts were analyzed using rapid qualitative analysis and a thematic matrix. Four investigators coded early transcripts, adjudicated conflicts, and reached a mean Cohen kappa of 0.80 before remaining transcripts were coded by validated coders.
- Gap theme: Patients and caregivers reported that finding relevant information and resources was time-consuming, frustrating, and difficult because information found independently often lacked applicability, trustworthiness, or clarity.
- Self-triage gap: Patients and caregivers lacked confidence in deciding the urgency of new symptoms or concerns and in knowing whom to contact, especially outside clinic hours.
- Desired mHealth features: Participants wanted centralized, curated, trustworthy information from gynecologic oncology experts; plain-language glossaries; cancer- and treatment-specific content; and anticipatory caregiver information about what to expect during chemotherapy, radiation, and symptom management.
- Tailoring and trajectory: Participants wanted timely recommendations tailored to personal needs, cancer type, treatment phase, and survivorship concerns. Reminder preferences varied, so customization and changeable reminder frequency were important.
- Professional and peer interaction: Participants wanted opportunities to interact with knowledgeable clinical experts and peer experts through the app, including moderated question-answer or chat features to preserve accuracy and support.
- App usage preferences: Patients and caregivers wanted private spaces to seek information, express concerns, and obtain support separately from the other dyad member. They also wanted granular control over what information could be shared with caregivers, patients, and other family members.
- Novel findings emphasized by the authors: The study highlights self-triage support for symptom urgency and dyadic privacy/sharing control as important design requirements for gynecologic cancer mHealth self-management support.
- HCD/accessibility details: Participants noted variable technology proficiency and suggested alternative support routes such as call centers for people who are not tech-savvy.

## Study Takeaway and Story

Campbell 2024 shows that a gynecologic cancer patient-caregiver app cannot simply be a shared portal or a generic education library. The app must support private and shared use at the same time: curated information, symptom-urgency guidance, expert and peer connection, trajectory-specific recommendations, and flexible sharing controls.

The source's design value is direct and condition-specific. Its evidence level is formative qualitative design evidence, not an outcome trial. It supports what future gynecologic cancer mHealth should include and evaluate, not claims that such an app improves caregiver or patient outcomes.

## Design Implications

- Provide curated, plain-language gynecologic oncology information with source governance.
- Include symptom-urgency guidance that helps users decide when and whom to contact, while preserving human clinical escalation.
- Include caregiver-facing anticipatory guidance about treatment phases and practical support tasks.
- Support trajectory-sensitive recommendations rather than static education.
- Provide moderated peer and clinical expert interaction where accuracy and safety matter.
- Allow patient-only, caregiver-only, dyadic, and family-sharing spaces.
- Make sharing granular, revocable, and changeable over time.
- Offer low-tech alternatives or human support routes for users with limited technology proficiency.

## Evidence Limits

- This is formative qualitative design evidence rather than intervention-effectiveness evidence.
- Sample was mostly White and recruited through a trusted gynecologic oncology practice; generalizability to non-White, rural, lower-resource, and lower-digital-literacy groups is limited.
- Focus groups included both patients and caregivers, which may have inhibited disclosure of some private caregiver or patient concerns.
- COVID-19 timing may have shaped interest in mHealth and remote support.
- Self-triage support should be designed as guidance and escalation support, not autonomous clinical decision-making.
- Do not treat desired AI/recommender functionality as evaluated AI evidence.
