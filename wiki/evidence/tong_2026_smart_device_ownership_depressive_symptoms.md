# Smart device ownership and depressive symptoms among older adults: evidence from China

## Source Role

Tong examines heterogeneous smart-device ownership and depressive-symptom associations in older Chinese adults. It is ownership and digital-inclusion evidence, not an integrated smart-home evaluation or caregiver-outcome study.

## Study Picture

- Data came from the 2018 and 2020 China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey. The pooled dataset contained 22,811 observations; 10,542 with missing key variables were excluded, leaving 12,269 observations.
- Mean age was 71.17 years; 37.2% had rural hukou status, 78.6% reported at least one chronic disease, and 41.0% owned at least one of eight measured device types.
- Monitoring devices were electronic blood-pressure monitors, cholesterol devices, smart bandwatches, and sleep monitors. Assistive devices were smart wheelchairs and audiobook devices. Care devices were smart cameras and smart speakers.
- The outcome was a nine-item self-reported depressive-symptom score from 0 to 18, not a clinical diagnosis.

## Main Findings

- In adjusted pooled OLS models, owning any measured device was associated with a 0.496-point lower depressive-symptom score; each additional device type was associated with a 0.238-point lower score.
- Monitoring-device ownership (beta -0.317) and assistive-device ownership (beta -0.505) had negative associations, while the combined care-device category had a positive association (beta 0.538).
- Device-specific estimates were negative for electronic blood-pressure monitors, smart bandwatches, sleep monitors, and audiobook devices; the smart-camera estimate was weak and its 95% CI crossed zero. Smart wheelchairs, cholesterol devices, and smart speakers had positive estimates, plausibly reflecting health, functional, or care-need selection rather than device harm.
- Sensitivity analyses broadly supported the ownership-breadth and monitoring-device patterns, but the fixed-effects estimate for any ownership was not statistically significant.
- Social network and social adaptation statistically mediated part of the ownership-breadth association; offline activity did not. The reported total indirect association was -0.056, about 23% of the total association. The mediation analysis used 12,249 observations rather than the main 12,269.
- Associations were generally weaker among older adults with functional limitations, emphasizing that ownership alone may not translate into benefit when access, usability, assistance, and context are mismatched.

## Evidence Limits and Conceptual Boundaries

- Device ownership did not measure actual use, frequency, duration, purpose, proficiency, quality, or integration into daily routines.
- The device taxonomy groups heterogeneous and sometimes broadly defined products; results should not be generalized from one device type to "smart technology" as a whole.
- Reverse causation, confounding by income, education, health, function, family support, and indication for device purchase remain plausible despite sensitivity analyses.
- More than 46% of pooled observations were excluded for missing key variables, creating possible selection bias.
- Data end in 2020 and do not describe current AI-enabled products. The Chinese social, digital, and care context limits transferability.
- The study supports associations, not causal claims that smart devices prevent or treat depression. Positive coefficients for care-related devices should not be interpreted as evidence that the devices worsen mental health.

## Wiki Interpretation and Design Implications

Evaluate function, support needs, and actual use rather than treating all smart-device ownership as one intervention. Associations of care devices with higher symptom scores may reflect purchase in response to need; they establish neither benefit nor harm. Retain primary storage in adoption/preferences/equity while cross-linking smart-home and wearable knowledge.

These implications support research and design, not diagnosis, treatment recommendations, or autonomous care decisions.
