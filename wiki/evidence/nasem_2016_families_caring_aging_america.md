# National Academies 2016 Families Caring for an Aging America

## Source Summary

The National Academies 2016 report is a foundational U.S. consensus report on family caregiving for older adults. It assesses prevalence, caregiver roles, health/economic impacts, caregiver supports, health care and LTSS interactions, and policy recommendations.

## Key Evidence

- Family caregivers include relatives, partners, friends, and neighbors whose care work is primarily driven by personal relationship rather than financial payment.
- Need for caregiving is not an inevitable result of aging. Functional limitation, cognitive impairment, mental health, and chronic health problems drive the need for help.
- The report estimated 17.7 million U.S. caregivers of older adults in 2011 in community or residential care settings other than nursing homes, including 8.5 million caring for high-need older adults.
- High-need older adults are defined as those with probable dementia or needing help with at least two self-care activities.
- Family caregiving is often long-duration: the report states that nearly 70% of NSOC caregivers had provided care for 2 to 10 years and 15% for more than 10 years at the time of survey.
- Family caregivers increasingly provide medical and nursing tasks at home, navigate fragmented health/LTSS systems, interact with many provider types, and serve as surrogate decision makers.
- Caregivers may be excluded from treatment decisions and care planning even while providers assume they will implement the care plan.
- The report calls for movement from person-centered care alone toward person- and family-centered care.

## Recommendations Relevant to This Wiki

- Routinely identify family caregivers and assess their needs in health care and LTSS.
- Reform payment and delivery systems to support caregiver engagement.
- Train providers to recognize, engage, support, and refer family caregivers.
- Fund and disseminate evidence-based caregiver support programs.
- Evaluate economic supports for working caregivers.
- Improve data infrastructure for caregiver monitoring, tracking, and reporting.
- Evaluate caregiver interventions in real-world settings across diverse populations, conditions, and outcomes.
- Support innovation around assistive technologies, remote monitoring, sensing, telehealth, and care coordination.

## Wiki-Relevant Interpretation

This report anchors the wiki's older-adult caregiving policy and systems layer. It supports caregiver identification, assessment, care integration, provider training, economic context, and technology innovation as research/design issues.

## Design Implications

- Digital health systems should model both the care recipient and the family caregiver, with explicit consent, access, and autonomy boundaries.
- Caregiver-facing tools should support identification, needs assessment, training, referral, and coordination rather than assuming caregiver capacity.
- Technology innovation should be evaluated for equity, access, implementation, cost, and real-world fit.

## Gaps and Cautions

- Policy details from 2016 may be outdated; current policy claims need re-verification.
- Technology mentions are innovation targets and policy rationale, not direct effectiveness evidence for specific digital health or smart home systems.
- Family-centered care must not override care-recipient privacy, dignity, or autonomy.
