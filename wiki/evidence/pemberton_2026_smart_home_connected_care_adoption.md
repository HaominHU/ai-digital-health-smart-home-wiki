# Pemberton 2026 Smart Home Connected Care Adoption

## Source Role

This mixed-methods analysis of a UK connected-care deployment shows that adoption of passive smart-home monitoring is shared and caregiver-mediated, not simply an individual older adult's technology decision. Perceived value was often relational: reassurance, independence, connectedness, and reduced family worry.

## Study and System

- A UK telecommunications deployment combined plug-in motion sensors, Amazon Alexa, and a caregiver application.
- The trial included 393 users; 91 completed the voluntary analytic survey after approximately six months.
- Respondents were 14% younger than 65, 16% aged 65-75, 43% aged 75-85, and 27% older than 85. Other demographic characteristics were not linked to the analytic data.
- Partial least squares structural equation modeling was combined with qualitative responses.

## Findings

- The model explained 67% of variance in behavioral intention.
- Perceived ease of use was associated with intention (beta=.075, p=.02), as were facilitating conditions (beta=.232, p<.001). Perceived usefulness and social influence were not statistically significant in the model.
- The reported heterotrait-monotrait value between perceived usefulness and behavioral intention was .890, above the paper's stated .85 threshold, so construct distinction should be interpreted cautiously.
- Participants described peace of mind, safety, independence, connectedness, and reduced family worry. Passive monitoring could reduce repeated checking, but users also raised reliability, setup, cost, confidence, privacy, and dependency concerns.
- Six design requirements were derived: tangible value; onboarding and family support; a minimalist interface; shared dashboards with granular permissions; trust features such as fail-safes and logs; and simple versus advanced modes.

## Wiki Interpretation

Connected-care adoption is dyadic or networked. Design must identify who installs, configures, receives alerts, interprets patterns, pays, troubleshoots, and has permission to see which data. Reassurance and reduced checking can be meaningful benefits, but neither proves safety or effectiveness. Shared dashboards require granular permissions, revocation, auditability, and clear limits on what absence of an alert means.

## Evidence Limits

- This observational secondary analysis cannot establish causality or intervention effectiveness.
- The 91 voluntary respondents were a selected subset of 393 trial users.
- Baseline digital literacy, caregiver characteristics, and linked demographic detail were unavailable.
- Results reflect a COVID-era deployment with one commercial partner in one country.
- Model results and qualitative themes should not be generalized to all connected-care systems or populations.
