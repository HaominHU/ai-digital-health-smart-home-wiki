# Zhai 2023 Evidence on Digital Health Interventions to Support Family Caregivers

## Source Role

This systematic review summarizes modern digital health interventions for family caregivers and evaluates the scope of human-centered design methods. It is useful as broad digital health caregiver support evidence and as an HCD/usability/accessibility rationale source.

## Review-Level Takeaway

Zhai 2023 is most useful to this wiki as a map of the modern digital caregiver-intervention landscape rather than as evidence for one specific tool. Its main contribution is showing that recent caregiver digital health interventions cluster around web resources, telemedicine, and mHealth; that interventions most often try to deliver education, real-time communication, monitoring/data collection, psychotherapy, and social connection/support; and that HCD is still unevenly applied, with much of the reported design work limited to usability and satisfaction evaluation.

The review-level takeaway is that digital delivery can expand access to caregiver support, but future AI or smart-home caregiving systems should not simply copy the existence of digital delivery. They need stronger caregiver and care-recipient participation, clearer accessibility and cultural/linguistic tailoring, better reporting of technology use and failure, and outcome evaluation that separates caregiver well-being from usability and engagement metrics.

## Source-Backed Points

- The review searched PubMed, CINAHL, Embase, Cochrane Library, PsycINFO, ERIC, and ACM Digital Library in July 2019 and January 2021.
- The review was limited to 2014-2021 studies of family caregiver interventions assisted by modern technologies.
- Forty studies from 34 journals, 10 fields, and 19 countries were reviewed.
- Extracted fields included patient conditions, caregiver relationships, technology delivery modes, HCD methods, theoretical frameworks, intervention components, and caregiver health outcomes.
- MMAT and GRADE were used to evaluate study quality and evidence quality.
- Digitally enhanced health interventions were reported to improve caregiver psychological health, self-efficacy, caregiving skills, quality of life, social support, and problem-coping abilities.
- Eighteen of 40 studies included some HCD methods; all 18 evaluated usability and satisfaction of the technology delivering the intervention.
- Future work should include marginalized caregivers, improve accessibility and usability, and tailor interventions to be culturally and linguistically sensitive.
- The review argues that informal caregivers should be included as an essential component of patient care and that caregiver psychosocial assessment should be supported.

## Included Technology and Intervention Taxonomy

The review identified three main technology domains:

- Web resources: static web resources; interactive web resources; web resources with expert human interaction such as learning-management systems; and web resources with non-expert interaction such as discussion boards, online groups, or other peer/community spaces.
- Telemedicine: two-way, real-time interaction between caregivers and educators, counselors, doctors, nurses, or other professionals.
- mHealth: mobile phones, tablets, or PDAs used for health services, information, and data collection. The review also reports text messaging in two studies and wearable technology in one study.

The review grouped intervention purposes into five categories:

- Education: self-help strategies, disease knowledge, hospital or medical-procedure familiarity, and caregiver-patient relationship support.
- Real-time communication: caregiver questions, decision-making support, and self-care consultation through live communication with a coach, therapist, coordinator, professional, or peer.
- Data collection and monitoring: continuous data collection, analysis, monitoring, and alerting. The review discusses assistive technology and telecare examples for older adults with dementia, including sensors and alerts for risks such as falls, gas leaks, movement, emergencies, and lifestyle changes.
- Psychotherapy: CBT or related stress, coping, and problem-solving support, often combined with coach feedback, support groups, or web/mobile delivery.
- Connection and support: peer or expert support, social media or password-protected discussion forums, and caregiver communities that exchange information and support.

For this wiki, these are digital health delivery and intervention-component categories. They should not be treated as proof that AI agents, smart-home platforms, wearables, or telecare systems are effective unless a specific included study is separately ingested and supports that narrower claim.

## Population, Condition, and Equity Scope

The included studies covered broad family caregiver contexts rather than one condition-specific caregiver population. Care recipients included adults and older adults in 21 studies, children and adolescents in 18 studies, and all-age care-recipient contexts in one study. Dementia is relevant because several included studies focused on dementia caregivers, but the review also spans cancer, pediatric conditions, traumatic brain injury, acquired disability, heart failure, and other conditions.

The source calls for more inclusion of caregivers from diverse backgrounds, especially marginalized communities, and for interventions that improve accessibility and usability while becoming more culturally sensitive and linguistically appropriate. The paper does not fully operationalize "marginalized caregivers" as a fixed taxonomy. For wiki use, treat that phrase as an equity and sampling gap tied to the source's stated concerns: underrepresentation of diverse communities, accessibility barriers, usability barriers, cultural tailoring, linguistic appropriateness, and digital health literacy.

Do not map this review as direct evidence for spinal cord injury, systemic sclerosis-associated Raynaud phenomenon, postpartum women, gynecological cancer-specific needs, AI-agent architecture, or smart-home effectiveness unless a specific included study is separately ingested.

## Outcome and Evidence-Quality Taxonomy

Caregiver outcome domains reported across included studies included psychological health, self-efficacy, caregiving skills, quality of life, social support, and problem-coping abilities. HCD-related measures included usability and satisfaction, with reported instruments such as SUS, technology acceptance-style satisfaction, app-quality measures, efficiency, error frequency, understandability, acceptability, learnability, engagement, functionality, aesthetics, and information quality in different included studies.

MMAT was used to appraise study quality and GRADE was used to assess certainty of evidence. The review reported 32 randomized controlled trials and eight quasi-experimental designs. This strengthens the source as a broad evidence map, but heterogeneity still limits translation into any single condition, technology, or implementation recommendation.

## Wiki Interpretation

For this wiki, this source supports digital health caregiver intervention design, HCD evaluation, accessibility and equity planning, and caregiver outcome measurement. It should not be used to claim that any AI agent or smart-home system is effective.

## Evidence Limits

- The review includes heterogeneous studies and technologies.
- HCD was present in fewer than half of the included studies and often focused on usability/satisfaction.
- Usability, satisfaction, feasibility, and acceptability are not clinical or caregiver outcome effectiveness by themselves.
- Limitations include English-language restriction, no gray-literature search, publication bias, self-selection bias, self-report bias, attrition, technology instability, low usage, and intention-to-treat challenges.
- The source supports digital health promise and design rationale, not autonomous clinical decision-making.

## What This Review Does Not Answer

- Which specific digital health modality is best for a given caregiver population, condition, or care trajectory.
- Whether AI agents, smart-home monitoring, wearables, or telecare systems are effective as caregiver interventions.
- Whether usability, satisfaction, engagement, or acceptability findings translate into caregiver health, care-recipient health, utilization, or cost outcomes.
- How to operationalize marginalized caregiver inclusion beyond the review's stated need for diverse backgrounds, accessibility, usability, cultural sensitivity, linguistic appropriateness, and digital health literacy.
- How digital interventions should be safely integrated into clinical workflows, home-care escalation pathways, or caregiver privacy governance.
