# Van Houtven 2011 Caregiver Intervention Framework

## Source Summary

Van Houtven et al. 2011 develops an organizing framework for informal caregiver intervention design and evaluation. The article uses a structured review of 121 U.S. caregiver intervention reports published between 2000 and August 31, 2009 to classify caregiving activities and caregiver/care-recipient outcomes.

## Key Evidence

- The framework separates baseline characteristics, caregiving activities, caregiver outcomes, and care-recipient outcomes.
- Caregiving activities include clinical skills and knowledge, psychological skills/resources, support seeking, and quantity of caregiving.
- Caregiver outcomes include psychological health, physical health, health care utilization, and economic status.
- Care-recipient outcomes include psychological health, physical health/disease management, health care or long-term care utilization, and economic status.
- In the reviewed intervention literature, caregiver psychological outcomes were common, especially depression and burden.
- Caregiving activities, quantity of care, caregiver utilization, and caregiver economic outcomes were much less consistently measured.
- The authors recommend measuring quantity and/or quality of caregiving, broader caregiver and care-recipient outcomes, and common outcome sets for cross-study comparison.

## Wiki-Relevant Interpretation

This source should guide intervention evaluation logic across future ingests. A caregiver intervention claim should specify its target, mechanism, caregiving activity, caregiver outcome, care-recipient outcome, and economic or utilization outcome when relevant.

## Design Implications

- Digital caregiver systems should label whether a module targets knowledge, clinical skill, self-efficacy, coping, support seeking, quantity/quality of care, caregiver health, care-recipient outcomes, utilization, or cost.
- Evaluation plans should avoid relying only on caregiver satisfaction or depression/burden when the system aims to change care coordination, care quality, cost, safety, or care-recipient well-being.
- Economic and utilization outcomes should be considered early if the intervention is positioned as scalable, cost-saving, or system-facing.

## Gaps and Cautions

- This article classifies measures and framework components; it does not judge intervention effectiveness.
- The review is U.S.-focused and covers studies through August 31, 2009.
- The reviewed interventions were heterogeneous in content, dose, and objectives.
