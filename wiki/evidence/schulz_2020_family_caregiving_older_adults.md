# Schulz 2020 Family Caregiving for Older Adults

## Source Summary

Schulz et al. 2020 reviews family caregiving for older adults as a major public health and psychological research domain. It frames caregiving as chronic stress exposure and synthesizes evidence on caregiver roles, prevalence, impacts, risk factors, and intervention research.

## Key Evidence

- Family members are a primary source of support for older adults with chronic illness and disability.
- Caregiving has become more common, longer, more intense, and more complex as population aging, chronic disease/disability survival, and fragmented health and social systems shift care work to families.
- Caregiving tasks often accumulate over time, moving from sporadic support and monitoring to IADL/ADL support, health and medical tasks, care coordination, advocacy, surrogate decision making, end-of-life care, institutional transitions, and bereavement.
- Caregiving impact is variable. Some caregivers experience confidence, meaning, closeness, or assurance of care quality, while others face distress, burden, social isolation, work strain, poor health, or safety concerns.
- Risk factors for adverse caregiver outcomes include high care intensity, dementia care, medical procedures, lack of perceived choice, perceived care-recipient suffering, caregiver health limitations, limited support, and challenging home environments.
- Technology-based caregiver interventions can deliver knowledge, social support, therapeutic strategies, monitoring, training, and performance support, but evidence remains mixed and issues of sustained use, accessibility, cost-effectiveness, system design, and integration with standard care remain unresolved.

## Wiki-Relevant Interpretation

This source supports a broad older-adult caregiver foundation. It should be used as a background and design-rationale source rather than as direct proof that any one caregiver technology works.

## Design Implications

- Caregiver supports should combine practical task support with emotional support.
- Interventions should be tailored to care-recipient condition, disease/treatment trajectory, caregiver risk level, and caregiving intensity.
- Technology-based supports need accessibility, reliability, meaningful access, and integration with formal care rather than isolated feature delivery.
- Evaluation should distinguish statistically significant effects from clinically meaningful improvements in quality of life, symptoms, social validity, and social/system outcomes.

## Gaps and Cautions

- Intervention evidence is uneven and often limited by study quality, small samples, selective reporting, and limited follow-up.
- Dementia caregiver findings should not be generalized across all caregiving contexts without qualification.
- Smart home and AI implications are design rationale only unless supported by additional technology-specific evidence.
