# Zhai 2026 Evidence on Smart Home Technologies for Ageing in Place

## Source Role

This systematic review provides direct source-backed evidence on smart home technologies for adults aged 65+ living at home. It is a stronger smart-home source than prior caregiver or digital health sources that only mentioned sensing, telehealth, or home technologies as design rationale.

Use it for recent evidence on smart-home purposes, outcome domains, implementation considerations, and evidence limits.

## Objective

The review aimed to synthesize evidence on the role of smart home technologies in enabling older adults aged 65+ to live safely at home, with attention to health outcomes, independence, healthcare utilization, and implementation considerations.

## Methods

- PRISMA systematic review.
- Retrospectively registered with OSF: `10.17605/OSF.IO/4ZJNC`.
- Six English-language databases searched: Cochrane Library, PubMed, Web of Science, PsycINFO, ProQuest, and CINAHL.
- Search dates: February and March 2025.
- Publication range: 2020-2025.
- Included quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods studies evaluating smart home technologies for older adults.
- Methodological quality was assessed with Joanna Briggs Institute checklists and the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool.
- Data were synthesized thematically.

## Review Scope

- 2,196 records identified.
- 433 duplicates removed.
- 291 full-text articles assessed for eligibility.
- 30 studies included.
- Included studies covered 10 countries.
- Study designs included 16 quantitative, 8 qualitative, and 6 mixed-methods studies.
- Many studies involved community-dwelling older adults aged 65+, with some focused on dementia or cognitive impairment, chronic conditions, or home-care recipients.
- Most studies evaluated ambient sensor or IoT systems; fewer evaluated voice assistants or socially assistive/interactive robots.

## Source-Backed Findings

- Smart home technologies were used for physiological monitoring, safety, functional and emergency detection, social interaction, cognitive support, sensory support, chronic disease management, and daily activity monitoring.
- Some studies reported reduced healthcare utilization, improved quality of life and independence, enhanced chronic disease management, and positive experiences for users and carers.
- Family and carer reassurance appeared as a recurring benefit.
- Integrated systems that combine monitoring, risk detection, and support functions appeared more promising than narrowly focused standalone technologies, though the evidence remains heterogeneous.
- Physiological monitoring and functional or emergency detection systems were more often associated with measurable clinical or service-related outcomes.
- Voice assistants, social interaction tools, and robotic technologies were more often linked to reassurance, companionship, usability, or acceptability than to sustained clinical or system-level impact.
- Barriers included privacy, cost, technical reliability, digital literacy, connectivity, setup burden, and fit with everyday home and care routines.
- Implementation should prioritize reducing user burden, supporting carers, and fitting existing care arrangements rather than adding isolated devices with poor contextual fit.

## Wiki Interpretation

For this wiki, the review supports smart home technologies as a reusable intervention lens for aging-in-place design, home monitoring, caregiver reassurance, chronic disease support, and home-care coordination. It also reinforces that smart-home design should consider privacy, consent, accessibility, digital literacy, reliability, technical support, and everyday-care integration.

The review should be used carefully: it supports potential and recent evidence patterns, not universal effectiveness.

## Evidence Limits

- Evidence is heterogeneous across technologies, populations, designs, outcomes, and follow-up durations.
- No meta-analysis was conducted.
- The review included English-language studies from 2020 onward, so earlier foundational smart-home work may be missing.
- The review was registered retrospectively.
- Many included studies were small-scale, short-term, or feasibility-oriented.
- Smart-home claims should remain technology- and context-specific rather than generalized to all home sensing or automation systems.

## What This Review Does Not Answer

- Which smart-home system is most effective for a specific condition or caregiver population.
- Whether smart-home technology reduces caregiver burden across contexts.
- Whether passive monitoring improves care-recipient autonomy, dignity, or safety in the long term.
- How to govern multi-user consent in homes with caregivers, family members, visitors, and care workers.
- Cost-effectiveness and implementation sustainability at scale.
