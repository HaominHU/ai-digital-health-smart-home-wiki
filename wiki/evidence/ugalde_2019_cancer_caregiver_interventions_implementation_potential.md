# Ugalde 2019 Cancer Caregiver Interventions Implementation Potential

## Source Role

Ugalde et al. 2019 is a systematic review of cancer caregiver intervention studies appraised through implementation outcomes. Use it as broad cancer caregiver intervention implementation-readiness evidence, especially for acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility, fidelity, cost, caregiver involvement, reach, targeting, workforce, and reporting gaps. Do not use it as gynecological-cancer-specific effectiveness evidence, AI-agent evidence, smart-home evidence, mHealth effectiveness evidence, autonomous-care evidence, or cancer-treatment evidence.

## Review-Level Picture

- Review question/objective: The review examined whether cancer caregiver intervention studies were designed and reported in ways that support implementation into practice, complementing prior reviews focused mainly on efficacy.
- Implementation framework: The authors operationalized six early-stage implementation outcomes from Proctor et al.: acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility, fidelity, and costs. Penetration and sustainability were excluded because they are longer-term implementation outcomes.
- Search logic: Searches were run in CINAHL Complete, MEDLINE Complete, and PsycINFO Complete on 2018-01-05, with no language or publication-date limits. Reference lists from eight recent systematic reviews were also searched.
- Screening scope: The database search yielded 7,183 records, reduced to 4,501 after duplicate removal. Full texts were reviewed for 42 papers from the main database search, with 27 included. Six additional papers were identified from other sources, for a total of 33 papers representing 26 studies.
- Inclusion logic: Included studies involved informal unpaid adult caregivers with active caregiving roles for adults with cancer; interventions were programs, supports, sessions, or resources directed toward caregivers to improve their own functioning or help them support patients; designs included at least two conditions with one control condition; and outcomes focused on caregivers.
- Exclusion logic: Excluded studies focused on spouses or family members without established caregiving roles, samples where at least 25% of patients had non-cancer conditions, interventions focused substantially on both patients and caregivers, and designs with multiple experimental conditions but no control condition.
- Study-design scope: Three quarters of included studies were randomized controlled trials. Most comparison conditions were treatment as usual; some used placebo controls.
- Population/condition scope: Informal caregivers of adults with cancer. Most studies involved cancer-only patient samples; two allowed a small proportion of patients with other chronic conditions. The review is broad cancer caregiver intervention implementation evidence, not gynecological-cancer-specific evidence.
- Intervention format taxonomy: Most interventions were delivered face-to-face to individual caregivers. Other formats included face-to-face group delivery, telephone delivery, caregiver home delivery, places convenient for caregivers, and one independently accessed internet intervention.
- Intervention content taxonomy: Common content categories included information provision, skills development, social support, individual or group therapy, and self-care. Many interventions had multiple content types.
- Delivery workforce: Nurses were the most common intervention deliverers, followed by social workers and psychologists.
- Theory/pilot reporting: Fewer than half of studies explicitly reported theoretical frameworks. About one third had previously piloted the intervention; many did not report prior piloting.
- Acceptability finding: Fewer than half of studies reported caregiver-perspective acceptability evidence. Most did not report acceptability from other stakeholders. Direct caregiver involvement in intervention development was rare.
- Adoption finding: No included studies reported intentions, agreements, or actions to implement the intervention in practice. Two papers noted adoption-related concerns, including health-care-provider reservations and longer-term sustainability concerns related to delivery costs.
- Appropriateness finding: All studies gave a rationale for why the intervention fit cancer caregiver needs, but very few targeted higher-need caregiver groups such as caregivers with high distress or sleep problems.
- Feasibility finding: Most screened caregivers were eligible, but less than one third of eligible caregivers consented on average. Among caregivers who enrolled, completion was generally high and withdrawal was low.
- Caregiver time burden: Caregiver time needed to complete interventions ranged widely, with a median of 180 minutes among studies reporting this information.
- Fidelity finding: Most studies appeared to deliver interventions as intended, but more than one third did not report fidelity information.
- Cost/resource finding: Cost reporting mainly concerned staff delivery time. Many studies did not report training and expertise needed beyond staff occupation. Additional resources included written materials, audio materials, DVDs, laptops or internet access, biofeedback devices, and home help aides.
- Equity/accessibility/HCD details: The review highlights limited caregiver input in intervention development and limited targeting of high-need or vulnerable caregiver groups. It does not provide a robust equity or accessibility analysis.

## Review-Level Takeaway and Story

### Why this review exists

Cancer caregiver intervention trials had accumulated enough evidence for prior efficacy reviews, but the field still lacked a clear view of whether those interventions could plausibly be implemented in routine practice. The review therefore shifts attention from "does an intervention work in a study" to "was the intervention designed and reported in a way that a service could adopt, deliver, and sustain."

### What the review is really about

This is a translation-to-practice source. Its contribution is an implementation audit of cancer caregiver intervention research. It asks whether studies captured the information a health service would need: whether caregivers and other stakeholders find the intervention acceptable, whether anyone intends to adopt it, whether it fits a real caregiver need, whether eligible caregivers actually enroll, whether it was delivered as intended, and what time, staff, training, and resources are required.

### What it lets the wiki say

- Cancer caregiver intervention studies often lack implementation-relevant design and reporting.
- Caregiver acceptability and caregiver involvement in intervention development were underreported or uncommon in the reviewed literature.
- Broad eligibility does not guarantee reach; eligible caregivers often did not enroll.
- High completion among enrolled caregivers should not be confused with population-level feasibility or reach.
- Implementation planning should include adoption partners, caregiver-centered development, high-need targeting, fidelity reporting, and delivery-cost/resource reporting.
- Proctor implementation outcomes provide a useful evaluation lens for caregiver intervention translation.

### What it does not let the wiki say

- It does not prove that a particular cancer caregiver intervention is effective.
- It does not prove that acceptability equals effectiveness.
- It does not show that digital, AI, smart-home, or autonomous caregiver support is effective.
- It does not provide direct gynecological-cancer caregiver evidence.
- It does not prove that included interventions were not implemented later; it mainly evaluates what was reported in intervention trial papers.

## Wiki Interpretation

Ugalde 2019 should be used as an implementation-readiness checklist for cancer caregiver intervention design. In this wiki, it pairs well with RE-AIM and Proctor-style implementation logic: reach, adoption, fidelity, delivery burden, workforce fit, cost, and maintenance cannot be inferred from efficacy, acceptability, or completion among enrolled participants.

For system design, the source supports asking whether caregivers helped shape the design, which high-need subgroup is being targeted, what caregiver time burden is acceptable, which workforce or service partner would adopt it, what training and resources are required, how fidelity is tracked, and how implementation cost is estimated. This is implementation-planning logic, not evidence that a digital system works.

## Design Implications

- Build caregiver co-design into cancer caregiver intervention development rather than relying only on researcher-defined needs.
- Distinguish eligibility, enrollment, completion, and representativeness when evaluating reach.
- Target high-need caregiver groups when the intervention burden is non-trivial.
- Document staff role, training, expertise, delivery time, and resource requirements.
- Track fidelity and dose delivered rather than assuming intervention packages are implemented as designed.
- Capture adoption partners and service setting requirements before claiming translation potential.
- Treat caregiver time burden as both an outcome and a reach/adoption barrier.

## Evidence Limits

- The review appraises implementation potential from published trial reporting; missing information does not always prove that implementation work did not occur.
- It excludes dyadic patient-caregiver interventions when the intervention focused substantially on both patients and caregivers, so it does not cover the full cancer caregiver support landscape.
- It is broad cancer caregiver evidence, not gynecological-cancer-specific evidence.
- It should not be used as direct effectiveness evidence for caregiver outcomes.
- It should not be used as evidence for AI agents, smart homes, mHealth, autonomous care, or clinical decision support.
- Table extraction from the PDF was artifact-prone; the integration relies on readable abstract, methods, narrative results, discussion, and conclusion text.
