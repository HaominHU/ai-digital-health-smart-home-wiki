# Age-Friendly Design and Optimization of Digital Health Technologies for Medication Adherence in Older Adults With Chronic Diseases: Scoping Review

## Source Role

Maps medication-support technologies, older-user barriers, and age-friendly design directions. The review offers a framework and evidence-gap map without validating the framework or estimating a pooled intervention effect.

## Study or Framework Picture

- The protocol was registered on OSF (10.17605/OSF.IO/ZA6F2). PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, CINAHL, SinoMed, CNKI, Wanfang, and VIP were searched in two phases: January 2013 to September 8, 2025, then an update on September 2, 2026 covering through August 2026.
- Eligible English/Chinese sources addressed digital medication-adherence support for adults aged 60 or older with chronic diseases, or separately reported older-adult subgroups, across care settings.
- Two reviewers screened and charted evidence using social cognitive and person-environment fit theories. Fifty sources from the initial search plus ten from the update yielded 60 included sources: 21 randomized trials, 19 secondary reviews/meta-analyses, and 20 other designs.
- No formal quality/risk-of-bias appraisal was performed. Source counts are not counts of independent primary studies; overlap between included reviews and trials was not established here.

## Main Findings and Contribution

- The mapped technology groups were basic media (SMS/telephone), interactive digital tools (apps/cloud platforms), interpersonal support (WeChat-based care/telepharmacy), context-aware devices (smart pillboxes/sensors), and an unspecified/mixed group excluded from category analysis.
- Research concentrated on daily medication routines. Other stages were sparsely covered: prescription 4/60, pickup 2/60, storage 1/60, refill 3/60. No technology category covered the whole medication journey.
- The four influence levels are individual capacity/literacy/motivation; technical usability/usefulness; social support/infrastructure; and program preferences, personalization, interactivity, and support intensity.
- Five proposed design dimensions map barriers to responses: perceptibility (multimodal accessible presentation), comprehensibility (simple information and controllable reminders), operability (usable navigation/targets), emotional warmth (encouragement and human connections), and security/interoperability (privacy and clinical-workflow integration).
- Only one included source focused on living alone, two on low-resource settings, and one on frailty/cognitive impairment. Usability was unreported in 49/60, nonstandard in 10/60, and formal standardized usability assessment in 1/60; none assessed bidirectional EHR integration.
- Seventeen sources did not specify adherence measurement. Six controlled studies reported nonsignificant between-group adherence differences. Their co-occurring design features do not establish why effects were absent. Alert intensity/overload and insufficient interpersonal support are design issues to investigate.

## Evidence Limits and Conceptual Boundaries

- The scoping synthesis includes secondary reviews and heterogeneous original studies without quality weighting or meta-analysis; do not treat the 60-source map as independent comparative effectiveness evidence.
- The five design dimensions are derived recommendations, not a validated scale, proven intervention components, or verified causal mechanisms. Emotional warmth or human-like check-ins do not establish superiority of AI interaction or substitutes for human support.
- Gray literature was not systematically searched, missing information was not requested from authors, and cost received limited analysis. Sparse vulnerable-subgroup evidence limits generalization to people living alone, with cognitive impairment, or in low-resource settings.
- Medication adherence is a care-recipient outcome; caregiver workload, preferences, and consent were not demonstrated. Chronic disease and polypharmacy are not normal aging, and this broad review is not specific evidence for each priority condition.

## Wiki Interpretation and Design Implications

**Evidence-informed design direction:** match presentation, task operation, support, reminder intensity, and data handling to measured user needs. Evaluate prescription-to-refill support separately from daily reminder use.

**Research questions:** test the framework with diverse older users and caregivers using validated usability measures, sustained use, adherence measures, support workload, privacy, and workflow outcomes. Medication advice, dose changes, and clinical interpretation remain under professional review; this source supports research and design only.

## Privacy and Safety

Use published aggregate results and generalized themes. Do not reproduce participant quotations or author contact information. Health and household data require consent, minimum necessary access, secure storage, retention limits, auditability, and attention to re-identification and unequal access. Proposed clinical alerts require validation, human oversight, and defined escalation responsibilities. This source is for research, design, and knowledge support.
