# Tu 2026 Digital Health Literacy Profiles in Older Adults With Coronary Heart Disease

## Source Role

This observational study identifies variation in reported digital health literacy among older adults with coronary heart disease (CHD) who can already use digital devices. It adds a person-centered profiling approach to the digital-inclusion evidence, with important selection, measurement, and reporting limits. Profile-tailored support is a proposal to test, not an evaluated intervention.

The source was supplied in the a la carte discovery lane. Topic synthesis is shared with monthly PubMed and other sources through `wiki/concepts/digital_inclusion_and_supported_use.md`; discovery channel does not determine evidence strength.

## Full Study Picture

- **Design and setting:** convenience-sampled cross-sectional survey in four community settings in Qingdao, China, October-December 2024. Community clinicians verified CHD diagnoses through health records.
- **Participants:** 371 adults aged 60-88 years, mean age 69.71 (SD 6.85); 214 men. The sample included 172 people reported as having additional chronic conditions, but it was not a dedicated multimorbidity cohort.
- **Selection:** eligibility required ability to perform digital activities such as messaging, browsing, or WeChat. Nonusers were excluded, as were people with severe/acute illness or significant sensory limitations preventing participation. The results omit an important digitally excluded population.
- **Data collection:** paper questionnaires, with researchers reading and recording answers for 245 participants (66.0%). Assistance used standardized instructions but may still affect responses. These are reported abilities, not observed digital-task performance.
- **Literacy measure:** the 15-item Older Digital Health Literacy Scale covers information acquisition/evaluation (9 items), interaction (3), and application (3), each rated on a five-point scale. Total range: 15-75; reported alpha: 0.941.
- **Other measures:** demographic/clinical characteristics and a 21-item Online Health Information Seeking Scale, with study alpha 0.804. Categorization loses information; printed information-seeking score intervals overlap at their cut points.
- **Analysis:** latent profile analysis (LPA) treated the 15 ordinal indicators as approximately continuous, using robust maximum likelihood in Mplus, class-invariant residual variances, and one- through four-profile models. Authors report random-start replication and within-class residual checks. Subsequent group comparisons and multinomial logistic regressions used most-likely profile assignments in SPSS.

## Findings and Model Selection

- Mean total reported literacy was 39.31 (SD 7.27). The authors labeled three profiles low, middle, and high, with reported counts 96, 188, and 87 and total-score means 29.70, 40.12, and 48.16 respectively. These labels are sample-derived and are not diagnostic thresholds.
- The retained three-profile model had entropy 0.897. The four-profile model had lower information criteria and higher entropy, but a nonsignificant LMR test and one small class. The authors favored three profiles for parsimony and interpretability. Entropy does not establish externally validated prediction accuracy or clinical utility.
- Table 7 reports associations of lower income, living alone, and lower online information seeking with low versus high literacy; lower income and less frequent health-related digital-device use with middle versus high literacy. CHD duration under three years was associated with lower odds of low versus high literacy.
- Table 7 reports rural residence for middle versus low literacy, with OR 1.930 (95% CI 1.104-3.373). Conflicting prose is documented below.
- The authors propose basic skill practice, progressive guidance, privacy education, peer support, and advanced engagement for different profiles. No support intervention was delivered or compared, and actual self-management, adherence, health benefit, caregiver workload, and aging-in-place outcomes were not established.

## Reporting Caveats

The following issues were checked against rendered Tables 1-3, 5-7 rather than inferred solely from text extraction:

1. **Profile percentages:** printed counts 96, 188, and 87 sum to 371, but the corresponding printed percentages 26.3%, 50.5%, and 23.2% do not match count/denominator arithmetic. Table 3 contains a separate model-proportion column. Preserve the counts with this caveat; do not silently replace percentages or conflate assigned class counts with model proportions.
2. **Living arrangements:** Table 1 category counts total 316 rather than 371. Table 5 labels the spouse row n=163, but its three profile cells sum to 218. The underlying distribution cannot be recovered reliably from the supplied PDF.
3. **Residence contrast:** the results paragraph describes urban residence and high literacy. Table 7 instead reports rural residence for middle versus low literacy, consistent with the later residence discussion. Retain the table-defined comparison and flag the conflicting narrative.
4. **CHD duration:** Table 7's under-three-years result compares low with high literacy. The prose expands this to high versus both other groups without presenting the corresponding middle-versus-high estimate. Do not repeat that expansion as established.
5. **Imprecise information-seeking estimate:** reported OR 14.054 (95% CI 1.373-143.853) for low information seeking in the low-versus-high comparison has substantial uncertainty. Authors note sparse data and possible quasi-separation. It is not a stable risk score.

These discrepancies constrain precision-dependent numerical reuse. A final-version check or author clarification is needed before relying on conflicting descriptive distributions or regression interpretations; the wiki does not silently repair the source.

## Evidence Limits

- Cross-sectional associations do not establish direction or causality. Device use and information seeking may influence reported literacy or follow from it.
- Convenience sampling, one Chinese region, exclusion of nonusers, and participation requirements limit transfer to all older adults, other countries, dementia, or people with substantial sensory/cognitive/functional barriers.
- Most-likely-class regression does not fully propagate classification uncertainty. The profiles need independent replication, assessment of model assumptions, and comparison with simpler continuous scores before operational use.
- Reported literacy is distinct from actual task completion, sustained use, health outcomes, and support received. Living arrangements are not a direct measure of family support or caregiver burden.
- Authors' explanations involving confidence, privacy concerns, information fatigue, or benefits of particular educational strategies are not demonstrated causal mechanisms or intervention effects in this study.
- CHD is a disease context, not normal aging. This is neither direct evidence for each priority wiki condition nor an AI or smart-home system evaluation.
- The supplied version is an article in press. Exact publication date and final volume/issue/article number remain unknown; acceptance date and PDF creation date do not establish publication date.

## Interpretation and Research Directions

**Cross-source interpretation:** together with Hwang's access/literacy study, Liu's perceived-fit work, and Long's medication-support review, this source reinforces the need to distinguish access, reported literacy, observed performance, assistance, and outcomes. These studies do not validate a causal sequence among those constructs. The sources are routed together through `wiki/concepts/digital_inclusion_and_supported_use.md`.

**Hypotheses to test:** determine whether profiles improve support matching beyond continuous scores and observed task assessment; validate classification across settings and over time; include nonusers and people without informal help. If families or paid helpers provide support, measure their time, consent, and workload alongside older-adult autonomy, actual use, and self-management outcomes. The paper does not show that family-mediated support is effective or necessary.

## Privacy and Research Boundary

Only aggregate published findings and generalized implications are retained. No participant quotations or author contact details are reproduced. Future systems require consent, data minimization, granular and revocable access, secure storage and retention, auditability, fairness assessment, and human oversight. This source supports research, design, and knowledge support; it does not authorize automated clinical triage, diagnosis, or treatment recommendations.
