# Becque 2023 Supportive Interventions for Advanced Cancer Caregivers

## Source Role

Becque et al. 2023 is a systematic review of supportive interventions for family caregivers of patients with advanced cancer. Use it as broad advanced cancer caregiver supportive-intervention evidence, especially for intervention taxonomy, target-population matching, caregiver outcome dimensions, and implementation/delivery considerations. Do not use it as gynecological-cancer-specific effectiveness evidence, AI-agent evidence, smart-home evidence, mHealth effectiveness evidence, or cancer-treatment evidence.

## Review-Level Picture

- Review question/objective: The review examined characteristics of supportive interventions for family caregivers of patients with advanced cancer, evidence for effectiveness across caregiver outcome dimensions, and whether intervention characteristics were associated with caregiver outcomes.
- Inclusion logic: Included English-language quantitative studies published from January 2004 through January 2020 that reported interventions to support adult family caregivers of patients with advanced cancer and included family caregiver well-being outcomes.
- Exclusion logic: Excluded patient-focused interventions, bereavement-specific interventions, pediatric cancer or mental comorbidity/cognitive impairment contexts such as dementia, conference abstracts, and pilot or feasibility studies.
- Search/screening: Searches used Embase, Medline Ovid, Web of Science, Cochrane Central, CINAHL, and Google Scholar. The review identified 16,713 records; after duplicate removal, 7,957 records remained; 87 full-text publications were reviewed; 35 articles reporting 32 studies met inclusion criteria.
- Study designs: Twenty-two included studies were randomized controlled trials. Other designs included quasi-experimental, stepped-wedge cluster non-randomized, single-group pre-post, and other quantitative designs.
- Population/condition scope: Family caregivers of adults with advanced cancer across care settings. Most studies included varied cancer diagnoses; a smaller number focused on lung cancer or breast cancer. The review is not direct gynecological-cancer-specific evidence.
- Caregiver relationship scope: Spouses were the most common caregiver group, but adult children, parents, and other family members were also represented.
- Quality appraisal: Included studies were appraised with the Hawker Quality Assessment Tool. The average score was 28.4 on a 9-36 scale, with scores ranging from 22 to 33. Ethical issues were reported less often than other quality domains.
- Intervention target-population taxonomy: Interventions targeted individual family caregivers, patient-caregiver dyads, families, or peer groups. Counts reported in the abstract were individual caregivers (n = 15), dyads (n = 11), families (n = 2), and peer groups (n = 4).
- Intervention content taxonomy: Intervention components were classified as patient caregiving, family caregiver self-care, and family care. Most interventions were multicomponent. Thirty-one interventions included caregiver self-care, 22 included patient-caregiving support, and 16 included family-care content.
- Theory and mechanism: Eleven interventions were based on Lazarus and Folkman's stress and coping theory; other interventions used diverse theoretical bases.
- Delivery modes and settings: Most interventions were delivered face-to-face, sometimes combined with telephone contacts. Three used telephone contacts only, and two were online/user-driven eHealth interventions. Many were home-based; others occurred in outpatient hospitals, inpatient hospitals, palliative care units, hospice, or general-practitioner office settings.
- Outcome taxonomy: Caregiver outcomes were classified into physical, psycho-emotional, social, spiritual, daily functioning, and quality of life dimensions.
- Main findings: Most interventions significantly improved at least one caregiver outcome. The abstract reports 29 interventions with beneficial effects, mostly in psycho-emotional outcomes (n = 24), daily functioning (n = 13), and social outcomes (n = 6).
- Target-population pattern: Individual interventions mainly improved psycho-emotional outcomes. Dyadic and family interventions improved psycho-emotional and social outcomes. Group interventions mainly improved daily functioning.
- Delivery-mode caution: The review did not find that mode, duration, dose, provider, setting, or content category clearly explained outcomes. It notes the value of face-to-face communication while also reporting positive effects from online and telephone interventions.

## Review-Level Takeaway and Story

### Why this review exists

Advanced cancer caregiving often occurs in palliative and end-of-life contexts where family caregivers provide practical care, medical-care support, household and financial help, psychosocial support, communication support, and advocacy. Supportive interventions had accumulated, but they differed in target group, delivery mode, components, theory, and outcome domains.

### What the review is really about

This review is a sorting and matching source. It organizes advanced cancer caregiver interventions by who is targeted, what support component is offered, how it is delivered, and which caregiver outcome dimension improves. Its core contribution is not a single best intervention; it is an intervention-to-outcome map.

### What it lets the wiki say

- Advanced cancer caregiver interventions are diverse and often multicomponent.
- Most reviewed interventions reported at least one beneficial caregiver outcome, especially psycho-emotional outcomes.
- Individual caregiver, dyadic/family, and group intervention formats appear to align with different outcome dimensions.
- Caregiver self-care is a dominant intervention component, alongside patient-caregiving and family-care content.
- Advanced cancer caregiver intervention design should match caregiver context, relationship structure, delivery burden, organizational capacity, and outcome target.

### What it does not let the wiki say

- It does not identify one universally superior advanced cancer caregiver intervention.
- It does not establish gynecological-cancer-specific intervention effects.
- It does not prove that online or telephone delivery is equivalent or superior to face-to-face support.
- It does not establish effectiveness for AI agents, smart-home systems, mHealth, or autonomous caregiver support.
- It does not replace Hartnett 2016 for end-stage ovarian cancer caregiver burden.

## Wiki Interpretation

Becque 2023 should be used as broad advanced cancer caregiver intervention taxonomy and outcome-mapping evidence. In this wiki, it can support advanced cancer context for the gynecological cancer condition overlay, but that mapping must stay cautious because most included studies were not gynecological-cancer-specific.

For system design, the source supports asking: is the caregiver's dominant need psycho-emotional support, daily caregiving competence, social/family functioning, spiritual support, physical strain support, or quality of life? The support format can then be matched to individual, dyadic, family, or peer-group delivery. This is a design rationale, not evidence that a digital system works.

## Design Implications

- Assessment should identify advanced cancer caregiver outcome targets before assigning intervention content.
- Modules can be organized around caregiver self-care, patient-caregiving support, and family-care support.
- Dyadic and family pathways should be considered when social or relationship functioning is the target.
- Group or peer pathways may fit daily-functioning support, but should not be assumed superior across all outcomes.
- Delivery burden matters: session number, duration, provider requirement, self-administered options, and care setting can affect implementation feasibility.
- Digital or AI-supported systems should use this source for intervention taxonomy and outcome targeting, not for digital effectiveness claims.

## Evidence Limits

- This is broad advanced cancer caregiver evidence, not gynecological-cancer-specific evidence.
- Included studies were heterogeneous in intervention content, target group, mode, dose, setting, and outcome measurement.
- The review found target population appeared associated with outcome dimension, while other intervention characteristics were not clearly associated with outcomes.
- Most studies were conducted in Western-oriented countries, limiting transferability.
- Ethical issues were reported less often than other quality domains.
- Table extraction from the two-column PDF was artifact-prone; the integration relies on readable abstract, methods, narrative results, discussion, and conclusion text.
- Do not treat telephone or online intervention signals as AI-agent, smart-home, or mHealth effectiveness evidence.
- Do not treat supportive-care intervention evidence as cancer treatment evidence or clinical decision support.
