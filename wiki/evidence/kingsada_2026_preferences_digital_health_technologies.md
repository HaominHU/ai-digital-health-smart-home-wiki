# Kingsada 2026 Evidence on Preferences Towards Digital Health Technologies

## Source Role

This scoping review synthesizes patient preferences for digital health technologies, including eHealth, telehealth, telemedicine, and mHealth. It is useful as broad patient-preference, digital-health adoption, health economics, and HTA-oriented design evidence.

This source belongs to the monthly PubMed source lane. It is not part of the `cg_system_core` staged caregiver system-design ingest.

## Review-Level Takeaway

Kingsada 2026 is most useful to this wiki as a broad preference-method and adoption-friction map for digital health technologies. Its main contribution is showing which DHT attributes patients tend to value and how preference evidence has been elicited across qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods studies.

The review-level design implication is that DHTs should not be treated as acceptable just because they are technically available. Preference-sensitive design should account for cost burden, privacy and data security trust, convenience, personalization, ease of use, clinician connection, and the option to combine digital support with in-person care.

## Source-Backed Points

- The review followed PRISMA-ScR guidance but did not register a protocol because the authors framed it as a scoping review rather than a systematic literature review.
- Searches covered PubMed, EMBASE, CINAHL, Scopus, and Web of Science for peer-reviewed English-language literature published from 2000 to 2026.
- The review excluded grey literature, commentaries, editorials, protocols, and conference abstracts.
- The database search identified 2,419 records; after duplicate removal and screening, 85 studies met inclusion criteria.
- Included studies comprised 27 qualitative studies, 45 quantitative studies, and 13 mixed-methods studies.
- Included study publication years ranged from 2005 to 2026, with 60% published from 2022 to 2026.
- Quantitative studies most often used attribute-based preference methods; discrete choice experiments were the dominant method.
- Six quantitative studies used contingent valuation to estimate willingness to pay for DHTs.
- Qualitative studies most often used interviews and focus groups, with thematic, deductive, inductive, and mixed analytic approaches.
- Studies addressed broad DHT characteristics and condition-specific contexts. Common clinical areas included mental health, cardiology, dermatology, arthritis, cancer, neurological disorders, sexual and reproductive health, asthma, diabetes, HIV, prenatal care, rheumatic diseases, addiction, and chronic diseases.
- DHT categories included mHealth, telehealth, telemedicine, and eHealth; mHealth was the most frequently studied category.
- Recurring preference domains included cost, privacy, time management, convenience, ease of use, utility, data security, personalization, interaction with clinicians, feedback, and hybrid digital/in-person models.
- Older-adult preference evidence was limited but suggested that older adults often value simple, clearly explained, tailored tools, multimodal education, initial face-to-face interaction, privacy, comfort, and clinical support.
- The review cautions that preferences vary by DHT type, health condition, age, user group, national reimbursement context, and healthcare-system setting.

## Preference-Methods Taxonomy

Quantitative preference methods included:

- Discrete choice experiments.
- Conjoint analysis.
- Best-worst scaling.
- Contingent valuation for willingness-to-pay estimation.

Qualitative preference methods included:

- Semi-structured interviews.
- Focus groups.
- Surveys used in qualitative designs.
- Combined focus group and interview approaches.
- Delphi methods.

Analytic approaches included thematic content analysis, deductive analysis, inductive analysis, mixed deductive/inductive approaches, and immersion-crystallization.

For this wiki, these methods are useful as design and evaluation options when a digital health study needs to understand trade-offs among cost, privacy, convenience, personalization, access, clinical support, human contact, and technical features.

## Population, Condition, and Technology Scope

The source is broad patient-facing digital health evidence. It is not caregiver-specific and should not be used as direct evidence for family caregiver needs, caregiver intervention effects, or caregiver outcomes.

Older adults are a relevant subgroup when the design question involves age, chronic condition management, telehealth, mHealth, technical barriers, privacy concerns, face-to-face support, or accessibility. The source should not be used to treat chronic disease, dementia, disability, or digital exclusion as normal aging.

Condition-specific content should remain source-level context unless a specific included study is separately ingested. Do not map this review as direct evidence for dementia, spinal cord injury, gynecological cancer, postpartum women, systemic sclerosis-associated Raynaud phenomenon, or falls.

## Wiki Interpretation

For patient-facing or caregiver-adjacent systems, this source supports preference-sensitive design. It suggests that tools should be easy to use, affordable, privacy-preserving, secure, personalized, and compatible with human support. It also supports eliciting patient or care-recipient preferences separately from caregiver preferences when systems involve shared access, proxy use, or family-supported digital care.

## Evidence Limits

- This is a scoping review of preferences, not an intervention-effectiveness review.
- Patient preferences are not the same as caregiver preferences, clinician preferences, or care-recipient outcomes.
- Preference heterogeneity is substantial across DHT type, condition, age, healthcare system, and reimbursement context.
- Grey literature, non-English studies, conference abstracts, protocols, commentaries, and editorials were excluded.
- The review does not formally appraise qualitative study quality.
- Cost findings are difficult to compare because payment vehicles and cost perspectives vary across studies.
- Preference evidence should not be treated as universally transferable across countries because reimbursement, regulation, infrastructure, and care models shape patient experience.
- Older-adult conclusions are relevant but limited because only a small number of included studies had average participant age over 60.
- The source supports research, design, HTA, and knowledge support; it does not support clinical decision-making.

## What This Review Does Not Answer

- Whether DHTs improve clinical outcomes, caregiver outcomes, care-recipient outcomes, utilization, or costs.
- Whether AI agents, smart-home monitoring, or caregiver apps are safe or effective.
- Whether patient preferences are aligned with caregiver preferences in shared-care or proxy-use systems.
- Which digital health modality is best for a specific condition, caregiver population, or care trajectory.
- How preference-sensitive design should be operationalized in home-care escalation, clinical workflow integration, or privacy governance.
