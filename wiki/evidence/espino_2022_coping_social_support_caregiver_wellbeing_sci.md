# Espino 2022 Coping Social Support and SCI Caregiver Well-Being

## Source Role

Ryerson Espino et al. 2022 is a mixed-methods study of coping, problem solving, social support, unmet needs, and well-being among family caregivers of adults with SCI. Use it for SCI caregiver protective-factor, unmet-need, social-support, and stratified assessment design rationale. Do not use it as intervention-effectiveness evidence or as a validated clinical triage model.

## Study-Level Picture

- Problem/gap: SCI caregiver research has emphasized burden and distress, while less work has examined protective factors such as coping, problem solving, social support, and how these relate to caregiver well-being and unmet needs.
- Population/setting: 39 caregiver-adult with SCI dyads from four rehabilitation hospitals in the United States, including one Veterans Affairs hospital.
- Sample picture: Caregivers were mostly female, racially and ethnically diverse, and included partners/spouses, parents, siblings, children, and extended relatives. Average caregiver duration was about 12 years. Adults with SCI were mostly male, many had tetraplegia and incomplete injury, and more than one-quarter were veterans.
- Methods: Concurrent mixed methods design with separate semi-structured interviews and standardized surveys for caregivers and adults with SCI. Interviews occurred before surveys. Follow-up member checking was attempted 12-15 months later.
- Measures/outcomes: Caregiver well-being domains included leisure time satisfaction, social integration, anxiety, depression, physical health complaints, caregiver burden, and quality of life. Qualitative domains included unmet needs, social support, adaptive coping, caregiving responsibilities, relationship dynamics, and future care plans.
- Key innovation: The study used mixed methods and exploratory caregiver profiles to connect protective-factor patterns with lived experience, unmet needs, and well-being differences.
- Main qualitative findings: Families described financial strain, limited emergency funds, difficulty obtaining supplemental support and therapies, insufficient insurance coverage, home and vehicle modification needs, complex caregiver compensation processes, social isolation, emotional strain, 24/7 care demands, respite gaps, and caregiver health issues.
- Social support findings: Support included emotional support, humor, spiritual support, instrumental support, home maintenance, assistance with employment and education tasks, fundraising, VA supports, home health aides, peer support, and caregiver-support programming.
- Adaptive coping findings: Caregivers and care recipients emphasized reframing SCI without allowing it to define the person or family, confidence to learn tasks, care-recipient independence, health routines, boundary-setting, mentoring others, and shared future care planning.
- Profile findings: Three caregiver profiles emerged: effective problem solvers with moderate social support and better well-being; mixed problem solvers with stronger negative orientation and mixed support, with more burden and red flags; and low endorsers overall, with lower support/problem-solving endorsement and qualitative isolation/unmet needs.

## Wiki Interpretation

This study supports stratified SCI caregiver support. It suggests caregiver systems should not only provide information; they should assess coping orientation, social support, respite and resource gaps, mental/physical health red flags, and future care planning needs. The profile findings are useful design hypotheses, not a ready clinical algorithm.

## Design Implications

- Add assessment fields for problem-solving orientation, negative/avoidant coping, social support satisfaction, isolation, respite access, resource navigation, caregiver health concerns, and future care plans.
- Route caregivers toward skill-building, peer/social support, respite navigation, mental-health referral, resource support, or planning resources based on need profile.
- Preserve dyadic planning: care-recipient independence and shared future plans matter, but caregiver needs and care-recipient needs remain distinct.
- Provide resource information across the rehabilitation spectrum and in multiple formats.
- Evaluate intervention effects on caregiver well-being, burden, social integration, leisure satisfaction, physical health complaints, and qualitative unmet needs.

## Evidence Limits

- Mixed methods findings are rich but not intervention-effectiveness evidence.
- Quantitative subgroup/profile sizes were small.
- Recruitment across four sites was challenging; the sample may not represent all SCI caregiving families.
- English-speaking and cognitively able participants were required.
- VA supports may not generalize to non-VA systems.
- The profile analysis is exploratory and should guide screening/design hypotheses rather than serve as a validated triage model.
- This source is not AI, smart-home, autonomous care, or digital intervention-effectiveness evidence.
