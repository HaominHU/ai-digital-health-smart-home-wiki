# Development of Guidelines for Implementing Digital Technology Into In-Home Aged Care: Mixed Methods Approach

## Source Role

Develops an implementation checklist for Australian in-home aged care. It adds client, workforce, and organizational context, while family carers were not directly sampled and implementation outcomes remain untested.

## Study or Framework Picture

- Two stages combined knowledge generation and guideline refinement through a research-institute partnership with one national Australian in-home aged-care provider.
- A narrative review with a systematic search used CINAHL, EMBASE, PubMed, and PsycINFO in March 2023, limited to English publications within the preceding ten years. Abstract screening used two reviewers; full-text screening used one reviewer because of resource constraints. Quality scores informed interpretation without formal weighting or score-based exclusions.
- Primary inputs were 267 staff surveys, 359 client surveys, and 18 staff interviews. Convenience or purposive recruitment limits representativeness; the staff response rate could not be calculated.
- An online workshop involved 10 staff plus a consumer representative. Iterative refinement and a single smart-glasses case study supplied feedback about the draft guidelines and checklist.
- Client surveys contributed preferences, but no direct client interviews and no direct family-carer surveys, interviews, or workshop participation were included.

## Main Findings and Contribution

- Seven domains comprise 19 subthemes: technology design (familiarity/ease, technical issues, infrastructure, interoperability); privacy/security/trust (privacy, reliability/trust); training/support (staff training, client training, IT support); organizational culture (readiness, confidence/acceptance, workload, communication); client care (preferences, suitability); digital literacy (staff self-efficacy/preferences, client/staff literacy); perceived benefits (usefulness, benefit awareness).
- Each subtheme has enablers, barriers, and practical tips. The checklist is intended for repeated use during implementation, including assessment of whether technology suits the intended outcome.
- Staff readiness was mostly moderate or high, but troubleshooting help and reliable technology remained important. Staff readiness and client acceptance are different constructs.
- Clients commonly had at least one technology at home (320/359, 89%), whereas 129/359 (36%) reported health-related technology use. Availability does not establish actual health-tool use or benefit.
- Workshop feedback emphasized backup systems. The smart-glasses case study prompted clearer benefit communication and locally relevant workforce training, especially for rural/remote contexts.

## Evidence Limits and Conceptual Boundaries

- Guideline development and case-study feedback do not establish adoption, fidelity, feasibility across providers, cost-effectiveness, improved care, or workforce savings. The abstract's validation language should be read as preliminary refinement of applicability.
- A single partner organization, one technology case, resource-constrained review methods, and convenience/purposive sampling limit transferability. Richer client qualitative input and direct family-carer involvement remain gaps.
- Reporting caveat: the staff-role description prints 43 care aides/assistants as 43% despite a total of 267. The client-survey paragraph also describes health-monitoring use as 176/359 (49%) after referring to a 129-person health-use subset; the denominators are unclear. Do not reuse these inconsistent detail estimates or silently correct them.
- Formal workforce and provider implementation needs are not direct evidence of family-caregiver needs, burden reduction, or clinical effectiveness.

## Wiki Interpretation and Design Implications

**Evidence-informed implementation direction:** evaluate client choice, staff confidence, training, connectivity, workload, leadership, technical support, and fallback processes together. Checklist use can organize planning, but its effect needs testing.

**Design direction to test:** include family carers directly when shared access or support duties are proposed, and measure whether tasks are added or reduced for clients, relatives, formal staff, and providers separately.

## Privacy and Safety

Use published aggregate results and generalized themes. Do not reproduce participant quotations or author contact information. Health and household data require consent, minimum necessary access, secure storage, retention limits, auditability, and attention to re-identification and unequal access. Proposed clinical alerts require validation, human oversight, and defined escalation responsibilities. This source is for research, design, and knowledge support.
