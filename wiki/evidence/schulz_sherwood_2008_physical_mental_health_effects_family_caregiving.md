# Schulz and Sherwood 2008 Evidence on Physical and Mental Health Effects of Family Caregiving

## Source Role

This article is a foundational caregiver health-effects framing source. It summarizes evidence linking informal family caregiving with psychological distress, impaired health behaviors, physical health effects, and mortality risk while also noting positive aspects of caregiving and research limitations.

## Source-Backed Points

- The article defines caregiving as care provided by a family member or friend rather than a reimbursed professional.
- Caregiving has features of chronic stress: sustained physical and psychological strain, unpredictability, uncontrollability, secondary stress across work and family relationships, and vigilance demands.
- Stress-coping models are used to frame caregiving stressors, caregiver resources or vulnerabilities, and caregiver health outcomes.
- Objective stressors include care recipient physical disability, cognitive impairment, behavior problems, type of care, and care intensity.
- Caregiver outcomes include psychological stress, impaired health behaviors, physiologic responses, psychiatric illness, physical illness, and mortality.
- Physical health measurement domains include self-rated health, chronic conditions, physical symptoms, medications, health service use, mortality, immune measures, stress hormones, cardiovascular measures, metabolic measures, wound healing, sleep, diet, exercise, self-care, and medical compliance.
- Psychological domains include depression, anxiety, stress or burden, subjective well-being, positive aspects of caregiving, and self-efficacy.
- Risk factors include behavior problems, cognitive impairment, functional disability, care duration and amount, vigilance demands, coresidence, caregiver age, caregiver sex, spousal relationship, socioeconomic status, prior health, and social support.
- Dementia caregiving is described as more challenging than physical-disability-only caregiving because of supervision needs, reduced reciprocity or gratitude, and higher likelihood of care recipient depression.
- Positive caregiving meanings can coexist with strain and may include feeling needed, meaning, skill development, and strengthened relationships.

## Wiki Interpretation

For this wiki, this source supports caregiver self-care, assessment, triage, and intervention evaluation logic. It should be used to justify tracking caregiver mental health, physical health, health behaviors, care intensity, vigilance, social support, and positive meaning as separate dimensions.

## Evidence Limits

- This is a concise review/framing article, not a new intervention trial.
- It should not be used as direct evidence for AI, telehealth, smart-home, or mHealth effectiveness.
- It should not be used to make precise prevalence or effect-size claims without checking the cited primary studies or meta-analyses.
- Dementia caregiving claims should not be generalized automatically to all caregiving contexts.
- Positive caregiving effects should not be used to minimize caregiver burden.
