# Wearable Health Technologies

## Scope

Wearable health technologies are body-worn or body-adjacent devices that collect, display, transmit, or respond to health, physiological, behavioral, or functional data. Keep this lens distinct from ambient smart-home sensing even when both feed a remote-monitoring service.

## Technology Categories

- Consumer activity, sleep, and wellness trackers.
- Clinical or clinical-adjacent physiological monitoring devices.
- Smart textiles, patches, biosensors, and rehabilitation devices.
- Human activity recognition and functional monitoring.
- Connected remote-monitoring ecosystems integrating telemedicine, the Internet of Medical Things, EHRs, or AI-supported analysis.

## Intended Support Roles

- Reduce manual tracking when passive collection is acceptable and valid.
- Support longitudinal awareness, rehabilitation, or symptom conversations.
- Help caregivers and care recipients prepare observations for clinical review.
- Trigger bounded follow-up workflows when the measure and response protocol are validated.

## Safety, Privacy, and Implementation Boundaries

- Distinguish consumer wellness signals from validated clinical measurements.
- Evaluate sensor validity, missingness, wear adherence, skin or comfort burden, accessibility, device maintenance, and representativeness across bodies and contexts.
- Minimize data, define who can access raw and interpreted data, support revocation, and retain auditability.
- Avoid converting passive sensing into continuous caregiver vigilance or automated clinical decision-making.
- Treat interoperability, training, workflow integration, and response ownership as part of the intervention.

## Evidence

- `2026-07-31_ding_wearable-healthcare-bibliometric-analysis`: A 12,812-article bibliometric analysis maps a rapidly expanding and increasingly connected wearable-healthcare field. It identifies research structure and emerging directions, not clinical effectiveness.
- `2026-05-18_hu_dissertation-family-caregiver-mhealth-app`: Wearable integration and passive monitoring were proposed as future directions for reducing caregiver manual reporting burden; this remains a speculative design direction until evaluated.

## Gaps

- Direct caregiver and care-recipient outcome evidence for specific wearable-supported workflows.
- Clinical validity and fairness across conditions, ages, disabilities, skin tones, body types, and home contexts.
- Long-term adherence, response burden, cost-effectiveness, governance, and workflow sustainability.
