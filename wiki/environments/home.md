# Home Environment

## Scope

The home is a primary setting for caregiving, smart home technologies, monitoring, safety concerns, and privacy-sensitive data collection.

## Issues to Track

- Environmental fit.
- Accessibility.
- Fall risk and hazards.
- Monitoring and sensing.
- Privacy among household members.
- Caregiver workload.
- Technology installation and maintenance burden.

## Evidence

- `2020_schulz_family-caregiving-for-older-adults`: Older adults' homes increasingly function as de facto clinical care settings where family caregivers perform medical or nursing tasks, use medical technologies, monitor symptoms and medications, and coordinate care.
- `2016_nasem_families-caring-for-an-aging-america`: Family caregivers may manage transitions from hospital to home, perform technical procedures and equipment-related care, and need training and system support.
- `2023-04-21_mohammed_sci-family-caregiver-experiences-ghana`: SCI caregivers in Ghana described hospital-based hands-on care and community/home support roles such as transport, cooking, errands, and household management. The study supports hospital-to-home caregiver support implications but is not a home technology study.
- `2008_elliott_problem-solving-videoconferencing-sci-caregivers`: SCI caregiver PST was delivered through home-based videoconferencing after in-home setup, supporting remote caregiver coaching as a home/community delivery model while noting attrition and technology-era limits.
- `2009_elliott_brief-problem-solving-training-sci-caregivers`: Brief PST plus education was delivered around inpatient discharge and follow-up in home or outpatient settings, supporting first-year SCI transition support rather than home technology effectiveness.
- `2016_smith_caregiving-services-sci-systematic-review`: SCI caregiving services were defined as home-based paid or unpaid supports for basic and instrumental ADLs, including personal care, household management, and financial management.
- `2019_conti_secondary-conditions-caregiver-burden-sci`: SCI secondary-condition routines such as bladder/UTI management and pressure-injury prevention can become home-care workload and burden drivers for family caregivers.
- `2022_espino_coping-social-support-caregiver-wellbeing-sci`: SCI families described home and vehicle modification needs, supplemental/respite care gaps, home health aide support, and 24/7 care demands as part of long-term home caregiving.
- `2016-04_hartnett_caregiver-burden-end-stage-ovarian-cancer`: End-stage ovarian cancer care can require complicated care and support at home, contributing to caregiver schedule disruption and financial burden.
- `2019_ugalde_cancer-caregiver-interventions-implementation-potential`: Cancer caregiver interventions occurred across health services, telephone, caregiver homes, and places convenient for caregivers; implementation claims require reporting adoption, feasibility, fidelity, staff time, training, and resources rather than assuming home or remote delivery is scalable.
- `2023_becque_supportive-interventions-family-caregivers-advanced-cancer`: Many advanced cancer caregiver supportive interventions were home-based or delivered through face-to-face and telephone contacts, but this supports intervention delivery context rather than smart-home or mHealth effectiveness.
- `2024_campbell_gynecologic-cancer-caregiver-mhealth-self-management-needs`: Gynecologic cancer patients and caregivers wanted mHealth support for day-to-day information, symptom urgency, treatment trajectory needs, and private or shared support, showing home/community self-management design needs rather than smart-home effectiveness.
- `2024_dave_cancer-caregiver-needs-patient-advocacy-groups`: As cancer care shifts outpatient and homeward, informal caregivers may manage medication, symptom monitoring, transportation, paperwork, and medical/nursing tasks, often without adequate training or support.
- `2001_glasgow_re-aim-framework-chronic-illness-management`: Home-based and technology-mediated interventions may improve reach by reducing travel or scheduling barriers, but still need adoption, implementation, and maintenance evaluation.
- `2010_gitlin_cope-dementia-home-based-intervention`: COPE provides randomized trial evidence for assessing dementia care-recipient capabilities, medical issues, home environment, and caregiver-identified concerns, then training caregivers to simplify tasks and support engagement.
- `2017_nichols_reach-dementia-caregiver-healthcare-costs`: REACH VA suggests integrated home-based primary care context may matter for dementia caregiver intervention cost and adoption logic.
- `2003_hepburn_savvy-caregiver-transportable-program` and `2022_hepburn_telesavvy-online-dementia-caregiver-program`: Savvy and Tele-Savvy show that dementia caregiver role training can be transported into community or remote formats, but delivery format changes reach and access constraints.
- `2026-06-24_zhai_smart-home-technologies-ageing-in-place`: Recent smart-home evidence positions the home as a site for physiological monitoring, daily-activity monitoring, functional/emergency detection, chronic disease support, social interaction, and cognitive support, with implementation constrained by privacy, cost, reliability, digital literacy, and fit with everyday routines.
- `2026-07-23_fritz_community-in-the-loop-smart-home-monitoring`: Low-income housing, multilingual communication, internet access, community partnerships, and trusted human response were integral to ambient-monitoring implementation; the home system was not an emergency service.
- `2026-07-15_pemberton_smart-home-connected-care-adoption`: Connected-care use in the home involved shared adoption, caregiver access, passive monitoring, reassurance, privacy concerns, and setup or reliability work.

## Design Implications

- Treat the home as a care environment, not just a residence.
- Account for accessibility, clutter, stairs, equipment, privacy among household members, caregiver capacity, and technology maintenance burden.
- Include care-recipient consent and dignity when home monitoring or sensing is proposed.
- Evaluate whether home-based tools reach high-need households and can be sustained without adding excessive caregiver or technical support burden.
- For dementia interventions, distinguish home-environment/task tailoring from smart-home sensing; COPE is home-based intervention evidence, not smart-home technology evidence.
- For SCI caregiver interventions, distinguish home-based telehealth coaching from smart-home sensing, AI automation, or SCI clinical treatment.
- For SCI caregiving services, distinguish home-based human support from smart-home technology or remote monitoring.
- For SCI home-care support, distinguish secondary-condition routine support, respite/supplemental care, home modifications, and caregiver well-being from smart-home effectiveness claims.
- For cancer caregiver interventions, distinguish home-based or telephone delivery from implementation readiness; adoption, fidelity, staff time, training, and cost still need evidence.
- For advanced cancer caregiver interventions, distinguish home-based supportive care delivery from smart-home sensing, digital automation, or cancer clinical treatment.
- For gynecologic cancer mHealth, distinguish home/community self-management support from autonomous clinical triage or smart-home sensing.
- For broad cancer caregiving, home support design should include practical task burden, transportation, paperwork, treatment adherence, symptom management, and advocacy-resource linkage.
- For smart-home aging-in-place designs, distinguish passive monitoring, active prompting, emergency detection, social/robotic interaction, and care coordination because these functions have different evidence strength, privacy risks, and response-workflow demands.
- Treat connectivity, housing infrastructure, household routines, language, privacy, visitors, and multi-user consent as part of system performance.
- Provide a visible way to pause monitoring, correct rules, revoke access, review logs, contact a trusted person, and understand what the system cannot detect.
- Budget and evaluate the human response layer rather than treating community workers, nurses, caregivers, or family members as free infrastructure.
