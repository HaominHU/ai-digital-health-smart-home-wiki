# Digital Distress

## Core Definition

Digital distress is stress arising when monitoring or care technology feels intrusive, confusing, unreliable, insufficiently personalized, or disconnected from language and cultural context, especially when responsibility between people and technology is unclear. It is an emerging implementation construct, not a clinical diagnosis.

## Contributing Conditions

- Unclear monitoring scope, consent, or data sharing.
- Irrelevant alerts, missed changes, confusing non-alerts, or unstable connectivity.
- Messages that do not match a person's language, literacy, preferred channel, or cultural expectations.
- Technology that transfers troubleshooting, interpretation, or response work to users without agreement or support.
- Unclear responsibility among the care recipient, caregiver, support person, community worker, clinician, vendor, and automated system.
- Inability to correct rules, pause monitoring, revoke access, or reach a trusted human.

## Design and Evaluation Implications

Assess distress, privacy comfort, perceived surveillance, alert comprehension, trust, responsibility clarity, and ability to opt out or reconfigure. Provide language-concordant human contact, explainable alert logic, clear escalation ownership, and honest explanations of what the system cannot infer. Treat nonresponse and withdrawal as possible implementation signals rather than simple user failure.

## Evidence

- `2026-07-23_fritz_community-in-the-loop-smart-home-monitoring`: A low-income, multilingual smart-home prototype study introduced digital distress through participants' experiences of intrusiveness, confusion, unreliable or poorly personalized monitoring, language and cultural mismatch, and blurred human-technology responsibility.

## Evidence Boundary

The construct currently rests on one source in this wiki. Its dimensions, measures, prevalence, discriminant validity, and transferability across smart homes, wearables, portals, telehealth, and AI systems need further study.
